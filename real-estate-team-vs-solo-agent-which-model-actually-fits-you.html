Real Estate Team vs. Solo Agent: Which Model Actually Fits You?

Home›For Experienced Agents›Team vs. Solo

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

There are really three models, not two, and conflating them is why this decision goes wrong. Joining a team trades a share of your commission for lead flow, systems and coverage, which suits an agent whose constraint is volume. Leading a team trades your selling time for recruiting, training and management, which is a different job that many excellent salespeople dislike. Staying solo keeps the whole commission and the whole workload, which suits an agent with a mature referral base and low overhead. None is more advanced than the others. The right one depends on which constraint is actually limiting you right now.

Key takeaways
  • Joining a team, leading a team, and staying solo are three distinct jobs with different daily work, not three steps of one ladder.
  • Joining a team makes sense when your constraint is lead volume. You are buying deal flow with commission share.
  • Leading a team makes sense when you are turning away business you cannot service. You are buying capacity with your own selling time.
  • Staying solo makes sense when your referral base already feeds you and your overhead is low. Nothing about it is a lesser choice.
  • Team lead is a business structure in Florida rather than a licensing step, so the question is whether you want the work, not whether you qualify.

This question usually gets framed as a step up: you sell solo for a while, then you join a team, then you lead one. That framing quietly assumes the models are ranked. They are not. They are three different jobs, and the reason agents end up unhappy is almost always that they moved into a model that did not match their actual constraint.

Start with the constraint, not the model

Before comparing anything, name what is actually limiting your business right now. There are only a few honest answers.

If you have capacity and not enough deals, your constraint is lead volume. If you are turning away business or working past the point of doing it well, your constraint is capacity. If you have plenty of both and are frustrated by what you keep, your constraint is economics, which is a split and expenses conversation rather than a model one. And if you are doing fine but feel you should be doing something more, that is not a constraint at all, and it is the most common reason agents make this change badly.

Each model solves exactly one of those. None solves all of them.

Joining a team: buying deal flow with commission

You give up a share of your commission in exchange for leads you did not generate, systems you did not build, and colleagues who can cover a showing when you are unavailable.

This is a genuinely good trade for an agent whose problem is volume, and especially for a newer agent who would otherwise spend two years learning by trial. A smaller share of more deals beats a full share of few.

Where it goes wrong is when the lead flow does not materialize at the promised level, or when the split is priced as though it will. The questions worth asking are specific: how many leads per agent per month, what conversion has looked like historically, and what exactly you are expected to do in return. A team that cannot answer those with numbers is selling an idea rather than a system.

Leading a team: buying capacity with your own selling time

This is the least understood of the three, because it looks like a promotion and functions like a career change.

Your day stops being about clients and starts being about people: recruiting agents, training them, setting expectations, splitting commissions in a way that feels fair, covering for people on vacation, and having the conversations when someone is not performing. Our pages on recruiting agents, splitting commission fairly, and setting expectations from day one cover the actual work.

Plenty of outstanding salespeople genuinely do not enjoy any of that, and there is no shame in finding out that you are one of them. Our page on what makes a real estate team fail is worth reading before you build one rather than after.

One clarification that matters in Florida: leading a team is a business structure rather than a licensing step, so a broker license is not automatically required. Our page on whether you need a broker license to start a team covers what actually applies. The barrier is not qualification, it is whether you want the job.

Staying solo: the choice that gets treated as a default

An agent with a mature referral base, a defined niche and low overhead is running one of the most profitable models in this business. Full commission after your brokerage split, no team economics, no management overhead, complete control over how you work.

Its real weakness is singular and worth taking seriously: there is no redundancy. If you are sick, injured or away, your business stops. Our page on what happens to a pending deal if an agent gets sick covers the version of this that actually bites people, and it is the strongest practical argument for either a team or a brokerage with real coverage.

What solo does not have to mean is unsupported. This is the distinction that gets lost: the alternative to a team is not isolation, it is a brokerage that provides the support a team would otherwise provide.

The option the two-way framing hides

Most of what an agent joins a team to get, leads, systems, training, coverage and someone to ask, is also available from a brokerage that actually invests in its agents, without the team commission split on top of the brokerage split.

That is worth putting plainly, because agents often join teams to solve a problem their brokerage should have been solving. If you are considering a team primarily for lead flow and mentorship, the fair comparison is not team versus solo. It is team versus a better-supported solo practice.

The specific things to compare: what lead generation the brokerage actually does, what training exists for experienced agents rather than new ones, whether there is transaction and administrative support, and whether management competes with you for listings. That last one matters more than it sounds, and our page on why a non-competing manager changes everything explains why.

On the income question

People want a number here and the honest answer is that the model does not determine income, production and economics do. A solo agent doing steady repeat business with low overhead can comfortably out-earn a team lead carrying salaries and marketing costs. A team member with strong lead flow can out-earn a solo agent starving for deals.

Our page on team lead versus solo agent income works the math, and our page on what agents really take home after fees covers why gross production is the wrong number to compare.

How to actually decide

Name your constraint. If it is volume, look at teams and at what a better brokerage would give you for free, and compare those two honestly. If it is capacity, look hard at whether you want the management job, and talk to someone who has done it. If it is economics, this whole decision is a distraction from a split negotiation. And if you have no constraint and simply feel you should be advancing, our page on the Florida real estate career path makes the case that staying put and getting better is a real answer.

For a new agent specifically, the decision usually arrives as a split offer rather than as a model choice. See should you join a team and give up commission for mentorship.
