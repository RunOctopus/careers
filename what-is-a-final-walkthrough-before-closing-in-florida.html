What Is a Final Walkthrough Before Closing, and What Should a Buyer's Agent Check?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Final Walkthrough Before Closing

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

A final walkthrough is the buyer's last chance, usually within 24 to 48 hours of closing, to confirm the property is in the same condition it was in when the contract was signed and that any repairs the seller agreed to actually got done. It is not a new inspection and not a chance to renegotiate the deal over something the buyer simply changed their mind about. A buyer's agent should walk in with a specific, practical checklist rather than a vague once-over, and should know exactly what options exist, delaying closing, negotiating a repair credit, or in rare cases walking away, if something is genuinely wrong when they get there.

Key takeaways
  • A final walkthrough exists to confirm two things: the home's condition hasn't changed since the buyer last saw it, and any repairs the seller agreed to complete were actually completed.
  • It is not a second home inspection and not an opportunity to renegotiate price or terms over issues that were already known, or over new preferences the buyer has developed since going under contract.
  • It's typically scheduled within 24 to 48 hours of closing, often the same day or the day before, once the seller has fully moved out.
  • A useful walkthrough checklist covers repair verification with proof of work, testing systems and appliances that convey, checking for new damage, and confirming the home was left in the agreed-upon condition.
  • If something is genuinely wrong at the walkthrough, the real options are usually a short closing delay, a negotiated credit or escrow holdback to cover the fix, or, in serious cases, treating it as a breach of the contract's condition requirements.

What a final walkthrough is actually for

A final walkthrough is the buyer's opportunity, shortly before closing, to confirm that the property they're about to pay for is the same property they agreed to buy. That's a narrower purpose than it sounds. It is not a second home inspection, and it is not a chance to reopen negotiations over cosmetic preferences, prior disclosures, or anything the buyer already knew about and accepted when they signed the contract. It exists to catch two specific kinds of problems: something has changed about the home's condition since the buyer last saw it, whether through damage, neglect, or something removed that was supposed to stay, or a repair the seller agreed to make as part of the negotiated contract was never actually completed, or was completed poorly.

When the final walkthrough typically happens

Most final walkthroughs happen within 24 to 48 hours of the scheduled closing, often the day before or the same morning. The timing matters for a practical reason: it needs to happen after the seller has fully moved out and, ideally, after any negotiated repairs have had time to be completed, but close enough to closing that there's no real window for anything new to happen to the home between the walkthrough and the buyer taking possession. Scheduling it too early, say, a week before closing, defeats the purpose, since a lot can change in a vacant or partially vacant home in a week. A buyer's agent should coordinate this timing directly with the listing agent once the seller confirms they'll be fully moved out, rather than defaulting to a generic day-of-closing slot that doesn't leave room to fix a problem if one turns up.

A real checklist for the walkthrough

A useful final walkthrough follows a specific list, not a general impression of whether the house looks fine. A buyer's agent should walk their client through checking:

Repairs, with proof. If the seller agreed to specific repairs during the inspection negotiation, verify each one was actually done, and ask for receipts or invoices from a licensed contractor where relevant, not just a visual check that something looks different.

Systems and appliances that convey. Turn on the HVAC system and confirm it's producing heat or cool air. Run the water at every sink, flush every toilet, and check under sinks for new leaks. Test the garbage disposal, the dishwasher, the oven and range, and any other appliance included in the contract. Test light switches and outlets in every room.

New damage. Walk every room looking specifically for anything that wasn't there during the inspection: new holes in drywall from moving furniture out, scuffed or damaged flooring, a cracked window, damage to doors or trim.

Included items still present. Confirm anything the contract specified as included, light fixtures, window treatments, a shed, appliances, is actually still there. Sellers moving out sometimes take things they assumed were personal property even when the contract said otherwise.

Utilities and access. Confirm utilities are on so systems can actually be tested. Confirm garage door openers, mailbox keys, and all house keys are present, and if there's a pool, confirm the equipment runs and the pool wasn't left in neglected condition.

General cleanliness and condition. Confirm the home was left broom-clean and free of trash or debris, consistent with whatever the contract specifies, and that no unauthorized personal property was left behind.

What the walkthrough is not for

It's worth being direct with buyers about this before they walk in the door: the final walkthrough is not the moment to ask for a lower price because they've since seen a similar home for less, and it's not the moment to raise concerns about something that was fully visible and known at the time the offer was written. Buyers sometimes get cold feet in the final days before closing and look for something, anything, to renegotiate over. A buyer's agent's job is to keep the walkthrough focused on its actual purpose, condition and agreed repairs, and to be honest with a buyer about the difference between a legitimate finding and a case of last-minute nerves.

What happens if something is actually wrong

When a real problem does turn up, walking away from the deal is rarely the first move and usually not the right one. More often, the situation gets resolved one of a few ways. If a repair the seller promised simply wasn't done, or wasn't done correctly, the parties can agree to delay closing by a day or two while it's finished, or agree to hold back funds in escrow at closing specifically earmarked to cover completing the work afterward, so closing isn't held up indefinitely over a fixable issue. If new damage turned up that wasn't there before, agents typically negotiate a credit to the buyer covering the cost of the repair, documented with an estimate. In more serious situations, where the home's condition genuinely doesn't match what the buyer contracted for, this can become a real dispute over whether the seller breached the contract's condition requirements, which is a conversation for the buyer's agent to bring to their broker and, where appropriate, for the buyer to bring to an attorney, rather than something to resolve informally on the spot.

A common scenario: the seller isn't fully moved out yet

One of the most frequent real problems at a final walkthrough isn't damage or a botched repair, it's a seller who simply hasn't finished moving out with the closing date already locked in. Furniture still in rooms, a garage full of items the seller hasn't decided whether to take, or a pool deck cluttered with belongings can all make it genuinely hard to tell whether the underlying home is in the condition it's supposed to be. A buyer's agent walking into this situation shouldn't treat it as an automatic crisis, but also shouldn't sign off on a walkthrough that couldn't actually be completed. The practical fix is usually a same-day or next-day re-check once the seller confirms they're fully out, sometimes with a small holdback or a post-closing occupancy agreement if the seller genuinely needs a few extra days, rather than letting an incomplete walkthrough get waved through simply because the closing is already on the calendar.
