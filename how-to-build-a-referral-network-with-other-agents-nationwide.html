How to Build a Referral Network With Other Agents Nationwide

Home›For Experienced Agents›Building a Referral Network

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Agents build a nationwide referral network by meeting agents in other markets at referral-focused conferences, staying in touch with past clients who relocate, and consistently sending well-qualified leads so the relationship pays off in both directions over time. It’s a long game built on trust, communication, and follow-through, not a single transaction.

Key takeaways
  • A referral network is built relationship by relationship, not through a single directory listing or app.
  • Referral conferences and professional associations put you in a room with agents actively looking to trade referrals.
  • Your past clients who move out of state are one of the most reliable, renewable sources of outbound referral opportunities.
  • The agents who get the most referrals back are the ones who communicate well and protect the sending agent's reputation.
  • A small, well-vetted group of trusted contacts nationwide is worth more than a huge list you don't actually know.

Why bother building a referral network at all?

Every producing agent eventually gets a call from a past client who’s moving to another state, or from a friend who needs an agent in a city you don’t serve. Without a network, that lead just evaporates, or worse, goes to a random name from an online directory. With a network, you refer it to someone you trust, collect a referral fee, and strengthen a relationship that sends business back to you later. A nationwide referral network turns those one-off moments into a recurring, low-cost source of leads that has nothing to do with your local advertising budget.

Where do you actually meet agents in other markets?

The most efficient path is referral-focused conferences and professional associations, where agents from around the country show up specifically looking to build reciprocal relationships. These events exist because referrals are a real, structured part of the business, not an afterthought. Beyond conferences, national and state Realtor associations, brokerage-affiliated networks, and even alumni or military-relocation groups can put you in front of agents who serve markets you don’t. The goal isn’t to collect business cards; it’s to have a real conversation, understand how someone works, and decide whether you’d trust them with your client.

What role do your past clients play?

Your own client history is a quieter but steady source of connections. When a past client relocates for a job, retirement, or family reasons, that’s a natural moment to ask where they’re headed and offer to connect them with a vetted agent there. Staying in light touch with past clients (a check-in call, a holiday note, a quick text when you see their name pop up) keeps you top of mind when they or someone they know is ready to move, whether that’s across town or across the country.

What actually makes agents send referrals back to you?

Reciprocity is earned, not assumed. Agents who consistently receive referrals back are the ones who respond quickly, communicate clearly throughout the transaction, and treat the referred client with the same care as their own. When you send a referral, you’re putting your name and your past client’s experience on the line; agents remember who protected that trust and who didn’t. Following up after the closing, whether it went smoothly or not, tells the other agent you’re paying attention and reinforces that the relationship is worth maintaining.

How big should your network actually be?

Bigger isn’t automatically better. A handful of agents you genuinely trust in the markets your clients tend to move to (relocation hubs, popular retirement destinations, or wherever your specific client base migrates) will outperform a sprawling list of names you barely know. Quality control matters more here than almost anywhere else in the business, since a bad referral reflects on you personally, not just on the agent who handled it. It’s worth actually checking in on how a referral went, either directly with your past client or through the receiving agent, so you know whether that contact belongs in your trusted circle going forward or should quietly be dropped.

How do you keep a growing network organized?

Once you’ve made a handful of good connections, the challenge shifts to remembering who they are, where they work, and when you last spoke. Many agents keep a simple spreadsheet or use their CRM to log contact information, the markets each agent covers, and notes on how past referrals went. A short annual check-in, even just a message asking how business is going in their market, keeps the relationship warm so it’s not a cold reintroduction the next time you have a client heading their way. The agents who treat this as an ongoing practice, rather than something they do once and forget, are the ones whose networks actually produce business year after year.

How do referral fees typically work between agents?

When one agent refers a client to another, the receiving agent generally pays a referral fee (often in the 20 to 30% range of their commission) once the deal closes, though the exact amount is always negotiated between the two agents up front, usually in writing before the introduction is made. Terms and typical percentages can vary by market and relationship, so confirm specifics directly with the agent or brokerage before making a referral, and keep the agreement documented so there’s no confusion once the transaction closes.

Keep growing your network from a stable base

A strong referral network is easier to build when you’re backed by a brokerage that gives you the visibility, tools, and reputation to make those connections in the first place. Agents outside the area are far more comfortable sending a client to someone affiliated with a brokerage they’ve heard of, and far more willing to trust that the referral will be handled well.
