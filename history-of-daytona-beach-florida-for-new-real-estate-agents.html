The History of Daytona Beach, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Daytona Beach

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Daytona Beach began in 1870 when Mathias Day Jr., an Ohio publisher, bought land on the Halifax River and built a hotel, only to fall behind on his mortgage and lose the property within a couple of years. Residents named the town after him anyway when it incorporated in 1876, and two of the twenty-six people who voted for that incorporation, Thaddeus S. Gooden and John Tolliver, were Black men, Tolliver among them a road contractor who built much of what is now Ridgewood Avenue. From there the town's identity split and multiplied: hard-packed sand that made it the world's land-speed capital and, eventually, the birthplace of NASCAR; Mary McLeod Bethune's one-room school for five girls that grew into a university; and a boardwalk economy built on purpose during the Depression. That layered, sometimes contradictory history, not just the beach itself, is why Daytona Beach still reads as several distinct towns wearing one name.

Key takeaways
  • Mathias Day Jr. built a hotel here in 1870 and lost the land to debt within a couple of years, yet the town that grew up around it was named Daytona in his honor when it incorporated on July 26, 1876.
  • Two of the twenty-six men who voted to incorporate Daytona in 1876 were Black: Thaddeus S. Gooden and John Tolliver, the latter a road contractor who built much of Ridgewood Avenue, now U.S. 1.
  • Three once-separate towns, Daytona, Seabreeze, and Daytona Beach, merged into a single city on January 1, 1926, which is the real reason downtown and beachside still feel like two different markets today.
  • The beach's hard-packed sand made it the world capital of land-speed racing from 1902 to 1935, and that same racing culture led directly to NASCAR's founding at the Streamline Hotel in 1947 and 1948.
  • Mary McLeod Bethune opened a school for five girls in 1904 with a dollar fifty; it became Bethune-Cookman University in 2007, the only HBCU in the country founded by a woman.
  • A deliberate, WPA-built Boardwalk and Bandshell in 1937 and a $25 million, privately funded Riverfront Esplanade completed in the 2020s are the same instinct a century apart: this city keeps choosing to build its own tourism identity rather than just accept whatever growth comes.

Before Mathias Day: the Halifax River's first residents

Long before anyone from Ohio saw the Halifax River, this stretch of Florida's east coast was Timucua territory, part of the Mocama-speaking peoples who lived along the St. Johns River system and the coastal lagoons for centuries. Spanish explorers documented Timucua settlements in the region as early as the early 1600s, and physical evidence of that habitation survives close by today, most visibly in the large shell middens preserved at Tomoka State Park, just north of downtown Daytona Beach. The Timucua did not survive as a distinct people past the colonial era, decimated by disease and conflict following European contact. No hotel, no racetrack, and no listing changes that this land had a long human history before 1870. An agent doesn't need to become a historian of it, but the city's story didn't start with a failed hotel deal, even though that's where its modern name comes from.

A hotel, a lost mortgage, and a town named after the man who lost it

Mathias Day Jr. was an inventor and newspaper publisher from Mansfield, Ohio, not a career land developer, when he bought property on the Halifax River in 1870 and built a hotel that a small community grew up around. In 1871 he bought roughly 2,144 acres from the previous owner's daughter for $8,000, putting $1,200 down. By 1873 the settlement had twenty dwellings, a mercantile store, and a post office, real signs of a town taking hold. But Day couldn't sell lots fast enough to keep up with his own mortgage payments, and within a couple of years he lost the land itself to debt. He never got to keep the town he'd started. Residents named it Daytona anyway when it formally incorporated on July 26, 1876, and Day is still counted as the city's founding father, a genuinely unusual origin story: a town named in honor of a man whose own real estate deal failed.

Two Black men among the town's twenty-six founders

The 1876 incorporation vote is worth knowing in more detail than most tellings give it. Twenty-six people voted to incorporate Daytona that year, and two of them, Thaddeus S. Gooden and John Tolliver, were Black men, present at the town's founding, not added to its story later. Tolliver went on to play a genuinely substantial role in building the town: between 1879 and 1881 the town council repeatedly awarded him road-building contracts, the largest in 1881 covering a portion of what became Ridgewood Avenue, Daytona's main thoroughfare and, later, U.S. Route 1. An agent showing a listing anywhere near Ridgewood Avenue today is standing on a road a Black founding citizen was paid by the town to help build, a genuine, documented fact that complicates the simpler story of who built this place.

Three towns become one city

For its first fifty years, what's now called Daytona Beach was actually three separate, independently incorporated municipalities: Daytona itself on the mainland side of the Halifax River, and Seabreeze and Daytona Beach on the barrier island across it. Local leaders, civic figure J.B. Kahn prominent among them, pushed for consolidation through the early 1920s, and the Florida Legislature passed Chapter 10468, abolishing all three governments and merging them into a single City of Daytona Beach effective January 1, 1926. The newly consolidated city had a population of about 15,000, roughly a third of it Black. This merger is the actual, documented reason downtown Daytona Beach and beachside Daytona Beach still function almost like two different towns under one name: they were two different towns, separated by a river, for the first half-century of their existence, and an agent who explains that history to a buyer confused by the divide is offering a real answer, not a shrug.

The Birthplace of Speed

Daytona Beach's sand isn't ordinary beach sand. It's hard-packed and flat enough to drive on, a genuine geological fact that turned the beach into the birthplace of automotive land-speed racing. The first organized car and motorcycle races on the beach ran in 1902, and for the next three decades the world's fastest drivers came here specifically because nowhere else offered a long, flat, firm enough surface. Fred Marriott set a landmark speed record just north at Ormond in 1906. On November 5, 1927, Major Henry Segrave became the first driver ever to officially top 200 mph, hitting 203.79 mph in the Sunbeam 1000 HP on the Daytona Beach Road Course. Sir Malcolm Campbell brought his Bluebird cars to the beach five times between 1928 and 1935, eventually averaging 276.82 mph, before the sport moved on to Utah's Bonneville Salt Flats once beach speeds had been pushed about as far as they could go. It was this era, and specifically city booster Jerome A. Burgman, that gave Daytona Beach the nickname it still markets itself under: the World's Most Famous Beach. For an agent, this isn't nostalgia. Parts of the beach are still legally open to vehicle driving and parking today, a direct, physical legacy of this specific history, and it's a detail buyers unfamiliar with the area are often surprised, and sometimes delighted, to learn.

From the beach to the banking: how NASCAR actually started here

Beach racing didn't stay confined to pure speed records. The first Daytona 200 motorcycle race ran on January 24, 1937, on a 3.2-mile beach-and-road course, with starting times literally set by the tide tables, since the course depended on the sand being exposed. The race ran annually through 1941, paused for World War II, and came back in 1947 under mechanic-turned-promoter Bill France Sr., who'd moved to Daytona Beach in 1935 and taken over running the course by 1938. That 1947 race drew 176 riders and filled every hotel room in town, forcing residents to host bikers in their own homes, an informal, genuinely community-level welcome that's part of why the event became an annual pilgrimage rather than a one-off. Later that same year, on December 14, 1947, France gathered other racers and promoters in the rooftop Ebony Bar of the Streamline Hotel, an art-deco building that had opened in 1941 a few blocks from the sand. After roughly two months of meetings there, the group formally organized NASCAR on February 21, 1948. The Streamline Hotel still stands and reopened as a boutique property in 2017, explicitly built around its role as NASCAR's birthplace. As beach racing outgrew what the sand could safely handle, France built a permanent 2.5-mile, high-banked speedway nearby, opening it in February 1959 with the first Daytona 500. An agent working this market will meet buyers relocating for the racing industry itself, not just retirees and vacationers, and that's a direct, traceable line back to a beach that happened to be hard enough to drive fast on.

Mary McLeod Bethune and the school that became a university

On October 3, 1904, Mary McLeod Bethune, the daughter of formerly enslaved parents from South Carolina, opened the Daytona Literary and Industrial Training School for Negro Girls with five students, each paying fifty cents a week, and a total of $1.50 to her name. In 1923 the school began merging with the Cookman Institute of Jacksonville, founded in 1872, a process finalized in 1925 as the Daytona-Cookman Collegiate Institute. It earned accreditation in 1931 and was renamed Bethune-Cookman College in her honor, then achieved university status in 2007. It remains, to this day, the only historically Black college or university in the country founded by a woman. This history isn't separate from the rest of the city's story, it runs directly alongside it: even as Bethune built one of the country's genuinely significant educational institutions in Daytona Beach, Jim Crow-era Florida banned Black residents from most public beaches by the 1920s, this city's own famous sand included. Bethune's response was direct: on December 9, 1945, she helped charter what became Bethune-Volusia Beach, a stretch of oceanfront in nearby New Smyrna Beach where Black Floridians could swim without restriction, reportedly saying the ocean was God's water, meant for everyone. A buyer or a colleague near what's now Bethune-Cookman University's Midtown campus is near a real, still-active source of civic identity and pride, and an agent who understands why should never treat it as a footnote.

The Boardwalk and the Bandshell: tourism built on purpose

Like DeLand's downtown and Flagler Beach's pier, Daytona Beach's oceanfront Boardwalk district didn't happen by accident. The Bandshell, a 48-by-114-foot coquina-rock amphitheater seating roughly 4,000, was built as a Works Progress Administration project between 1936 and 1938, the city contributing $84,000 and the federal government $184,000. It was dedicated on July 4, 1937, and drew a reported crowd of 25,000 that day for a full slate of beach races, a ballgame, a downtown parade, and the concert itself. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1999 and remains the anchor of the Boardwalk today. For an agent, the Boardwalk and Bandshell district is the specific stretch of the city built, deliberately, as a Depression-era public investment in tourism, and it's still the part of Daytona Beach that functions most like a resort strip: dense, vacation-rental-heavy, and distinct from the residential neighborhoods just a few blocks inland.

Downtown's long, unfinished redevelopment

Downtown, on the mainland side of the Halifax River along historic Beach Street, tells a different and more recent story: decades of underinvestment followed by a real, still-in-progress turnaround. The most visible recent piece is the Riverfront Esplanade, a $25 million project largely funded by longtime residents Cici and Hyatt Brown, replacing a deteriorated riverfront park with a dog park, splash pad, botanical garden, and walking trails along Beach Street between International Speedway Boulevard and Orange Avenue. The first phase opened in June 2022 and the full esplanade in 2023. It sits alongside new private investment, including a new headquarters tower and residential construction along the river. None of this is finished. For an agent, that's the useful, honest framing: downtown Daytona Beach is a market in active transition right now, not a completed product, and buyers betting on it are betting on a redevelopment story that's still being written.

Daytona Beach today, by the numbers

The city's population was 72,283 at the 2020 census and has continued growing since. The median age is around 40 (about 39 for men, 42 for women), younger and more middle-aged than nearby Flagler Beach's retiree-heavy profile. Median household income runs in the low $50,000s, with a poverty rate near 20 percent, both figures reflecting a genuinely working-class, tourism-and-service economy core rather than a wealthy resort town. The population is roughly 54 percent White and 30 percent Black, with smaller Hispanic, Asian, and multiracial populations rounding it out, a meaningfully more diverse city than either DeLand or Flagler Beach. None of this is trivia for an agent: it shapes what price points, financing conversations, and neighborhood expectations actually look like here, as distinct from the postcard version of Daytona Beach that tourism marketing sells.

Who's actually from Daytona Beach

Bob Ross, the painter and television host, was born in Daytona Beach on October 29, 1942, though he spent most of his childhood growing up in the Orlando area, so it's a genuine birthplace claim, not a hometown one. Vince Carter, by contrast, is a real Daytona Beach story start to finish: born here in 1977, raised here, and a graduate of Mainland High School, where he was a McDonald's All-American in basketball, led the school to a state title, and was also, less famously, the school band's drum major. Carter went on to a 22-year NBA career widely regarded among the greatest dunkers the sport has produced. For an agent, getting the distinction right, born here versus raised here, matters, the same way it does with DeLand's Chipper Jones and Jacob deGrom: it's an honest, verifiable talking point either way, but only one of these two actually grew up in the neighborhoods a buyer might be touring.

Where Daytona Beach eats

The Ocean Deck Restaurant & Beach Bar has served the oceanfront since 1940, one of the city's oldest continuously operating restaurants and a real link to the pre-Speedway era of the town. Downtown's historic Beach Street corridor has filled in with independent, locally owned spots: the Dancing Avocado Kitchen for made-from-scratch breakfast and lunch, and Arepita Beach for Venezuelan food. The Cellar occupies what was once President Warren G. Harding's winter home, now an Italian restaurant built inside a genuinely historic residence rather than a themed replica. For an agent, a downtown with this kind of real, independent dining, not just beachfront chains, is a legitimate lifestyle selling point for buyers weighing Daytona Beach against a more manicured coastal town.

What there actually is to do in Daytona Beach

Beyond the Boardwalk, Bandshell, and beach itself, Daytona International Speedway still runs tours and events year-round for the racing-minded, and the Streamline Hotel's preserved bar and memorabilia offer a tangible, walkable piece of NASCAR's actual founding story. Bethune-Cookman University's campus anchors Midtown with real, ongoing cultural and academic life, not just historical significance. Downtown's Riverfront Esplanade adds a genuinely new, walkable green space along Beach Street. Volusia County's broader tourism economy, over 10 million visitors and roughly $5 billion in visitor spending countywide in 2023, supports a real, sustained restaurant, hospitality, and entertainment base that goes well beyond Spring Break and Bike Week, even though those two events remain the city's best-known annual draws.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer choosing a Boardwalk-district condo, a Midtown bungalow near Bethune-Cookman, or a quieter street off Ridgewood Avenue is responding, whether they know it or not, to decisions and accidents going back to a failed 1870s hotel deal, a 1926 merger of three towns, a beach hard enough to break land-speed records on, and a one-room school that became a university. An agent who can explain why downtown and beachside still feel like different towns, why the Streamline Hotel matters beyond being a nice old building, and why Bethune-Cookman is a source of real civic pride is offering something a generic listing sheet never can: a real answer to why this city is shaped the way it is. That's a genuine, durable edge with buyers who are choosing Daytona Beach on purpose, and most of them, once you explain it, will realize they are.
