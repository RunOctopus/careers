The History of Pierson, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Pierson

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Pierson started in 1876 as Piersonville, a railroad stop named for Peter Pierson and the small group of Swedish settlers, including family and cousins, who came to farm the land in Florida's far northwest Volusia County. Citrus and lumber carried the early economy until the devastating freezes of 1894 and 1895 wiped out the groves. In 1904, Peter Pierson planted a shipment of 10,000 asparagus plumosa fern plants sent by a relative in Connecticut, and it thrived under the area's natural oak shade. That one decision built an entirely different economy: at its peak, Pierson grew roughly 80% of the world's ornamental ferns, and cut foliage from Pierson-area farms is still a real, roughly $80 to $100 million industry today, still run in real part by the same founding families. Pierson incorporated as a town in 1926. It has never been a beach town or a suburb, and an agent who understands why matters more here than almost anywhere else in the county.

Key takeaways
  • Pierson began in 1876 as Piersonville, a railroad stop settled by Peter Pierson, his family, and other Swedish immigrants who farmed the land in far northwest Volusia County; the town incorporated in 1926.
  • The freezes of 1894 and 1895 destroyed the area's citrus groves, forcing the local economy to find something else, the same pattern that reshaped much of inland Florida agriculture in that era.
  • In 1904, Peter Pierson planted 10,000 asparagus plumosa fern plants sent by a relative in Connecticut. Growers later discovered the ferns thrived under natural oak hammock shade, and an entirely new industry was born.
  • At its peak, the Pierson area produced roughly 80% of the world's ornamental ferns, earning the town its enduring nickname, the Fern Capital of the World.
  • The fern industry is not a museum piece. It is still a real, roughly $80 to $100 million business today, still largely run by multi-generation founding families, and it still shapes how land in and around Pierson is used, zoned, and valued.
  • Pierson's buyers are not chasing beach access. They are drawn to rural land, agricultural property, and small-town character, and an agent who treats Pierson like a smaller version of a coastal market will misread the market entirely.

Before any of it: the St. Johns River corridor

Like the rest of Volusia County, the land around Pierson sits inside the St. Johns River Valley, home to the Mocama-speaking Timucua peoples for centuries before European contact. Pierson itself sits inland, but it borders Lake George, the second-largest lake in Florida and a widened stretch of the St. Johns River, a waterway that shaped Native settlement patterns across this entire region long before any township existed. The Timucua did not survive as a distinct people past the Spanish colonial era, decimated by disease and conflict. No fern farm, no rural homestead, and no listing in Pierson changes the fact that this land had a long human history before 1876. An agent doesn't need to become a historian of it, but the area's story didn't start with a railroad stop.

Piersonville: a Swedish settlement on the railroad line

Pierson's documented modern history begins in 1876, when Peter Pierson, along with family members and a small group of other Swedish settlers, came to this corner of northwest Volusia County to farm. The settlement they built became a stop on the new rail line, and as local history tells it, a railroad conductor arriving at the still-unnamed stop asked the first five men he met for their names. All five answered “Pierson.” The conductor named the stop Piersonville, and the name stuck in a shortened form for the town that followed. Whether or not every detail of that story is precise, the underlying fact is not in dispute: this was a real, small Swedish farming settlement, built along a rail line, named for the family that founded it. The town grew slowly through the 1880s on citrus, lumber, and fishing, the same mix of industries much of inland Florida depended on at the time. It would take a disaster to turn Piersonville into the Fern Capital of the World.

The freeze that ended the citrus economy

Like DeLand and much of the rest of central Florida, Pierson staked its early economy heavily on citrus. That bet failed in a single, brutal stretch: the freezes of December 1894 and February 1895, remembered across the state simply as the Great Freeze, destroyed citrus groves from Ocala to the coast, Pierson's included. Growers who had built their livelihoods around orange groves were left with dead trees and no income. For a town this small, with this little economic diversity to fall back on, that wasn't a setback, it was close to an extinction event for the local economy. What happened next is the actual reason Pierson has a distinctive identity today instead of just fading into the surrounding farmland.

10,000 fern plants from Connecticut

In 1904, Peter Pierson received a shipment of 10,000 asparagus plumosa fern plants from a relative running a hothouse florist operation in Connecticut, who thought the delicate ornamental fern, already popular with florists and funeral directors up north, might grow well in Florida's climate. Pierson planted them on a quarter acre under a slat-house structure, with string trellises the plumosa climbed before being cut, coiled with moss, and shipped north in newspaper-lined boxes. The plants didn't just survive. Growers soon discovered something better than the slat houses: the ferns thrived even more under the natural shade of Florida's oak hammocks, a discovery that let growers expand cultivation cheaply across land that was otherwise only good for scrub and citrus. Other Pierson farmers took notice, and what started as one man's freeze-recovery experiment became the region's defining industry within a generation.

Becoming the Fern Capital of the World

The fern business grew fast. Trains, and later trucks and planes, carried Pierson's delicate greenery to florists across the country, where it filled out bouquets, wreaths, and funeral arrangements as the finishing touch buyers rarely noticed but florists couldn't do without. At its peak, the Pierson area supplied roughly 80% of the world's ornamental fern greenery, an extraordinary concentration of a single specialty industry inside one small rural pocket of Florida. The nickname Fern Capital of the World isn't civic boosterism. It's a documented statement of market share that this town, at its peak, genuinely held. In 1926, with the fern economy established and the town's population and infrastructure grown enough to warrant it, Pierson formally incorporated. That same year, a new school building went up, doubling as the town's high school; the Town later bought that same historic building in 2017 to use as its town hall, a real, physical thread connecting Pierson's 1926 incorporation to its government today.

Leatherleaf, air freight, and a second generation of innovation

The fern industry didn't stand still after its first boom. In the 1940s, grower J.C. “Fox” Mayfield introduced a new variety, leatherleaf fern, which was slow to catch on at first but by the 1970s had overtaken asparagus plumosa as the dominant crop, thanks to a longer shelf life and less shedding, real practical advantages for florists shipping product long distances. Around the same time, Raiford G. Hagstrom, of the family that still runs one of Pierson's largest fern operations today, recognized the commercial upside of the crop and built out large-scale acreage, then pioneered air freight shipping to Canadian buyers in 1947, replacing slower rail delivery and opening genuinely international distribution decades before that was common for a Florida agricultural product. By the 1970s, growers had added polyurethane shade structures, modern irrigation, and freeze-protection sprinkler systems, the same “igloo effect” technique citrus and strawberry growers use elsewhere in Florida, where a thin layer of ice actually insulates the plant beneath it from harder freezes. None of this was static heritage. It was, and still is, a working agricultural business continuing to modernize.

Labor, migration, and a history worth telling honestly

Pierson's fern industry has always depended on real labor, and that history deserves an honest accounting, not a footnote. A 1970s housing boom pulled workers toward construction jobs elsewhere in Florida, creating a labor shortage on the fern farms. Growers responded by recruiting workers from Texas, many of them Mexican and Mexican-American, offering year-round employment that was, at the time, more stable than seasonal farm work elsewhere in the state. That recruitment built the foundation of Pierson's Hispanic and Latino community, which by the 2020 Census made up roughly 47% of the town's population, nearly equal with its non-Hispanic white population, a genuinely distinctive demographic makeup for a small rural Volusia County town. The Farmworker Association of Florida, a statewide advocacy organization with more than 10,000 member families, has maintained a Pierson location for decades and was part of the successful push to include fern cutters under the federal Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, which gave a real legalization path to undocumented farmworkers already living and working in the fern industry. This isn't background trivia. It's the reason Pierson's population and community character look the way they do today, and an agent working here should understand it as real, lived local history rather than skip past it.

The Benlate crisis

The fern industry hasn't been a story of unbroken growth. In 1991, the agricultural fungicide Benlate, widely used by Florida growers including Pierson's fern farms, was found to cause serious crop and soil damage. The resulting litigation led to a $214 million settlement in 1994, and Pierson's growers, like many Florida ornamental and nursery operations, saw real acreage losses in the aftermath. It's a documented reminder that the fern business, like any agricultural industry, carries real financial risk tied to chemicals, weather, and disease, not just steady, guaranteed income.

Pierson's fern industry today: still real, still working

The fern business in and around Pierson is not a preserved relic. It's an active, roughly $80 to $100 million industry today, and northeast Volusia County, centered on Pierson, still supplies around 80% of the cut greenery sold in florist bouquets across the United States and Canada. Multi-generation family operations still run much of it: Albin Hagstrom & Sons, descended from the same Hagstrom family that pioneered air freight in the 1940s, is now on its fourth generation, farming nearly 500 acres, growing roughly 80 varieties of greenery, and shipping around 180 million stems a year to all 50 states and destinations as far as New Zealand and Singapore. Smaller family operations like Shaw Lake Farms and Ronald Jones Ferneries still work the same land their families have farmed for generations. In 2016, growers organized the Floral Greens Farmers of Florida, a marketing cooperative built around the same crop that saved the town's economy in 1904. The industry also faces real, current pressure: anthracnose, a fungal disease, has damaged crops badly enough that some growers are experimenting with diversifying into other crops, including grapes, and hard freezes, like the one that threatened the industry's crucial pre-Valentine's Day harvest in January 2026, remain a genuine annual risk that growers actively manage with monitoring systems and sprinklers rather than take for granted. For an agent, this matters directly: agricultural land around Pierson isn't idle acreage waiting to be subdivided, much of it is active, working fernery land, shaded under oak hammocks, with real infrastructure, irrigation systems, and family ownership behind it, and that changes how it should be shown, priced, and discussed with a buyer.

Pierson today, by the numbers

Pierson's population sits at roughly 1,500 to 1,700 people depending on the estimate, a genuinely small town by any measure. The 2020 Census put the population at 1,542, with a Hispanic or Latino population of about 47%, nearly matching the non-Hispanic white population also around 47%, a legacy of the farmworker recruitment that built the modern fern industry. Median age runs in the mid-40s, older than a typical family suburb, and median household income is modest, in the mid-$50,000s, reflecting the town's agricultural economic base rather than a commuter or retiree market. None of this is trivia. It tells an agent plainly that Pierson is not a scaled-down version of DeLand or Deltona. It's its own kind of market, small, working, and agricultural, with a genuinely different buyer profile than almost anywhere else in the county.

Chipper Jones and Pierson's one genuine claim to national fame

Pierson's best-known name outside Volusia County is Hall of Fame Atlanta Braves third baseman Larry “Chipper” Jones, who was born in nearby DeLand but raised in Pierson, where his father coached at T. DeWitt Taylor Middle-High School, the same school that still serves Pierson's students today. The town's Chipper Jones Family Sports Complex, a 16-acre park with ball fields and a rodeo stadium, carries his name, a real and specific hometown connection rather than a borrowed one. It's a small but genuine point of pride for a town this size, and worth knowing when the subject comes up with a buyer or a client.

Why this history is the whole pitch for an agent in Pierson

Pierson will never be sold the way Ormond Beach or New Smyrna Beach is sold. There's no coastline, no boardwalk, no beach-access premium to lean on. What Pierson actually offers a buyer is rural land, working agricultural property, small-town scale, and a genuinely distinctive local identity built on more than a century of a single, real, still-operating industry. A buyer looking at acreage near Pierson, whether they want to farm it, build on it, or simply live somewhere quiet with room to breathe, is choosing something specific, and an agent who can explain why this land is shaded in oak hammocks, why fern trucks share the road with school buses, and why the workforce and community here look the way they do, is offering something a generic listing sheet never can. That's not a smaller version of the pitch used elsewhere in Volusia County. It's a genuinely different one, and it starts with actually knowing this history rather than treating Pierson as an afterthought on the way to somewhere else.
