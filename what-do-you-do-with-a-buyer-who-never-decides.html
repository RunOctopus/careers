What Do You Do With a Buyer Who Never Decides?

Home›For Experienced Agents›The Buyer Who Never Decides

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

You stop showing houses and find out what is actually stopping them, because after a certain number of viewings the problem is no longer the inventory. Usually it is one of five things: they cannot afford what they want, they are not truly ready, two people want different outcomes, they are frightened of committing, or nobody has ever defined what they are looking for. Each has a different response, and none of them is another Saturday of showings. If the real answer turns out to be that they are not buying this year, saying so kindly is better for both of you than another six months of weekends.

Key takeaways
  • After roughly ten to fifteen viewings without an offer, the problem is the buyer's situation and not the houses.
  • Five usual causes: budget mismatch, not actually ready, two people disagreeing, fear of committing, or no defined criteria.
  • Stop showing and have a proper conversation. More viewings reinforce the pattern rather than breaking it.
  • Ask what was wrong with the best one they saw. The answer tells you which problem you have.
  • Stepping back is a legitimate professional decision, and doing it kindly usually keeps the relationship.

Every agent has had one. Twenty, thirty, forty properties, always a reason, never an offer. Weekends disappear, mileage accumulates, and the buyer remains enthusiastic and entirely uncommitted.

The instinct is patience, on the theory that the right house will eventually appear. Occasionally that is true. Usually the pattern is telling you something specific.

When to stop treating it as normal

There is no exact number, but somewhere around ten to fifteen viewings without an offer, the explanation stops being the inventory and starts being the buyer's situation.

Two other signals matter more than the count. If they have seen properties that genuinely met their stated criteria and still passed, the criteria are not the real criteria. And if the objections keep moving, so the one that was too small is followed by one that was too far and then one where the kitchen was wrong, you are watching avoidance rather than evaluation.

The five things that are actually happening

1. They cannot afford what they want. The most common by a distance. Their budget buys one thing and their expectations were formed by another, often a house a relative owns or a listing they saw in a different market. Nobody has said this out loud, so they keep looking for a property that does not exist at their number.

2. They are not actually ready. A lease that runs until spring, a house that has not sold, a job that might move, a relationship decision that has not been made. They are looking because looking is pleasant and free, and the real timeline is months away.

3. Two people want different things. A couple where one wants the schools and the other wants the commute, or one is enthusiastic and the other is being carried along. Every property fails because it cannot satisfy both, and the disagreement is never stated in front of you.

4. They are frightened. Entirely reasonable. It is the largest purchase of their life, and if they lost money on a house before, or watched someone who did, the fear is specific rather than vague. The endless search is a way of participating without committing.

5. Nobody has defined what they are looking for. Sometimes this one is yours. If the search began with three bedrooms and a budget and never got more precise, they are evaluating each house against a feeling rather than a standard, and a feeling can always find an objection.

The conversation, and the question that diagnoses it

Stop booking showings and ask for a proper conversation, in an office or over coffee rather than in a driveway. Frame it as helpful rather than as a complaint, because it is: you want to find them a house and something is not working.

Then ask the question that does most of the work: of everything you have seen, which was the best one, and what specifically stopped you offering on it?

The answer sorts the problem for you. If they name a fixable objection on a house they liked, the criteria need work. If they cannot name a best one, they may not be ready. If the two of them name different houses, you have found the disagreement. If the objection is money, you are in the affordability conversation. And if they say they just were not sure, that is fear, and it deserves a direct and kind response rather than another viewing.

Two follow-ups worth having ready. What would a house need for you to offer this month, which forces specificity. And is there anything that has to happen before you could buy, which surfaces the lease, the sale or the job.

Then respond to the actual problem

If it is money, revisit the numbers honestly and involve their lender. Either the budget rises, the expectations change, or the area changes. Showing more properties at the same price does none of those things.

If they are not ready, say so without judgment and agree a realistic point to restart. Keep in touch properly in the meantime, because a buyer who felt respected when they were not ready comes back to you when they are. That is the long game described in building a sphere of influence.

If two people disagree, get it into the open gently by asking each of them separately what matters most. Naming the disagreement is often enough for them to resolve it, and it is not your job to arbitrate it.

If it is fear, be concrete rather than reassuring. Walk through what the inspection period allows, what happens if something is found, and what protections exist in the contract, per Florida's AS IS contract explained. Fear responds to specifics far better than to encouragement.

If the criteria were never defined, that is fixable and partly on you. Rebuild the brief properly: must-haves, would-likes, absolute exclusions, and what they would trade for what.

When to step back

You are allowed to decide that this is not a good use of your year, and new agents are far too reluctant to.

Do it well. Say honestly that you do not think you have found the right approach for them, that you are happy to reconnect when their situation is clearer, and offer to introduce them to a colleague if they would prefer someone else. That is a professional conversation, not a rejection, and people frequently respect it.

Be honest with yourself about the arithmetic too. Every weekend spent on a buyer who will not transact is a weekend not spent on finding a listing or on the network that produces the next client. That trade is the same one described from the seller side in what to do when a seller insists on overpricing.

What prevents it next time

Most of this is avoidable at the start of the relationship rather than at showing twenty-two.

Have a proper first conversation before any viewing: what they want, what they can spend, what has to happen before they could buy, and who else is involved in the decision. Ask what would make them offer on a house, and listen for whether the answer is specific. Establish the written arrangement between you early, which is covered in can you show a listing from another brokerage, because a documented relationship makes an honest conversation later much easier to have.

The honest bottom line

Forty showings is not a patience problem, it is a diagnosis problem. Stop showing, ask which house was the best and what stopped them, and respond to the answer rather than to the symptom.

If the answer is that they are not buying this year, say so kindly and stay in touch. That is a better outcome for both of you than another six months of Saturdays.

One practical note on the indecisive buyer: they signed something before you ever showed them a house, and what the written buyer agreement commits both of you to is worth rereading before month six.
