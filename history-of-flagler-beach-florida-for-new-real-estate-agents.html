The History of Flagler Beach, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Flagler Beach

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Flagler Beach started as a name, literally. Isaac I. Moody, a homesteader, platted what he called Ocean City Beach in 1909 on 169 acres he’d claimed, intending to build a beach resort before the railroads made it reachable. When he tried to get a post office in the 1920s, the U.S. Post Office Department told him Ocean City was taken, so he renamed the town Flagler Beach in honor of Henry Flagler, the railroad baron whose Florida East Coast Railway made this part of the coast developable in the first place. The town incorporated on April 16, 1925, and then made a choice that still defines it: strict building height limits and a commitment to preservation over resort-style tourism. That deliberate restraint, not just geography, explains why Flagler Beach became Florida’s Last Beach Town.

Key takeaways
  • Flagler Beach was originally platted as Ocean City Beach in 1909 by Isaac I. Moody, who homesteaded 169 acres intending to build an oceanfront resort.
  • The town was renamed Flagler Beach in the early 1920s when the Post Office Department informed Moody that the name Ocean City was already taken, so he named it after Henry Flagler, the Florida East Coast Railway magnate.
  • Flagler Beach was officially incorporated on April 16, 1925, by a vote of 41 to 10, with George Moody serving as the first mayor.
  • The 1928 pier, built just three years after incorporation, became the town’s defining landmark and remains largely intact today, a tangible connection to the roaring 1920s.
  • From its incorporation onward, Flagler Beach chose strict building-height restrictions and preservation over the high-rise resort development that reshaped Daytona Beach and other coastal Florida towns.
  • The choice to remain small and deliberately preserved is not nostalgia, it is a documented, repeated, community decision, and it is the reason the town still feels fundamentally different from other Florida beach towns.

Before settlement: Timucua territory and Spanish colonial beginnings

Like the rest of central Florida’s Atlantic coast, the land that became Flagler Beach was inhabited by the Timucua peoples long before European settlement. Spanish explorers documented Timucua settlements in the region as early as the early 1600s, and the St. Johns River valley, which runs through what is now Flagler County, was a significant corridor of Mocama-speaking Timucua population. No European permanent settlement occurred here during the Spanish colonial period; the coast remained mostly wilderness from the Spanish era through the early American territorial period. An agent showing property in Flagler Beach is selling land that had centuries of Indigenous habitation before the town’s 1909 founding.

Ocean City Beach: a homesteader’s dream in 1909

The modern history of what became Flagler Beach began with Isaac I. Moody and the Bunnell Development Company in 1909. Moody homesteaded 169 acres of beachfront and began plotting a town he called Ocean City Beach, marketing it as an oceanfront resort destination. Railroads were coming to central Florida, tourism was finding the coast, and Moody’s timing placed him in the right place at the right moment. But resort development in 1909 moved slowly without reliable rail access, and Ocean City Beach remained a small, undeveloped town through the 1910s and early 1920s.

A postal name collision changes everything: the rename to Flagler Beach

By the early 1920s, Ocean City Beach was growing enough that residents wanted to formalize a post office and mail delivery. When Moody applied to the U.S. Post Office Department for a postal permit, he learned that the name Ocean City was already registered in Florida for another town. The Post Office wouldn’t grant the permit under that name. Rather than fight it or invent a new generic name, Moody renamed the town Flagler Beach, in honor of Henry Morrison Flagler, the railroad magnate and oil baron whose Florida East Coast Railway had made the development of this stretch of coastline possible at all. The railroad brought tourists, capital, and connectivity. Naming the town after Flagler was both a practical solution to a postal problem and a genuine acknowledgment that this town existed only because Flagler’s railroad made it reachable. For an agent, that origin story matters: Flagler Beach’s identity from the start was tied to a deliberate connection to something larger, not just a generic plot of oceanfront land.

Incorporation on April 16, 1925

After years of informal growth, Flagler Beach formally incorporated on April 16, 1925, by a vote of 41 to 10 in favor. The first town officials elected were George Moody as mayor (likely a relative of Isaac Moody, the original developer) and councilmen Charles Parker, Harry Wallace Sessions, Robert W. Raulerson, Dewey D. Moody, and Luther O. Upson. That 41 to 10 vote margin suggests a unified community ready to govern itself, not a contentious split. The town immediately began planning its infrastructure and identity. Less than a year later, Flagler Beach was already beginning construction on the 1928 pier that would define its character for the next century.

The 1928 pier: from wooden pilings to century-old landmark

Construction of the Flagler Beach Municipal Pier began in 1927 and was completed in 1928, just three years after incorporation, making it one of the town’s first major public works projects. The Weir & Benson Company of Jacksonville built the pier to a length of 656 feet (some sources indicate it extended to 800 feet including a boardwalk), supported entirely by wooden pilings driven into the seafloor. For a small beach town of fewer than a thousand residents, the pier was transformative. It was the largest structure in Flagler Beach, visible from miles up and down the coast, and became the most photographed landmark almost immediately. The pier transformed the town’s identity from a quiet residential beach retreat to a tourist and fishing destination that could compete with larger Florida beach towns. That the town made this investment early, and has maintained it through nearly a century of hurricanes and damage, reflects a real commitment to the town’s future as a destination, not just a place to live.

The 1920s boom and the choice to resist high-rise development

The 1920s were Florida’s first real land boom, and Flagler Beach participated. New buildings went up, the pier and boardwalk brought visitors, and the town’s economy grew. But what happened next was the real turning point. As the boom continued into the 1930s and then into the postwar era, cities like Daytona Beach, 30 miles south, and Miami and Miami Beach further down the coast, chose to embrace high-rise resort development, condominiums, hotels, and the full tourist-economy model. Flagler Beach, intentionally, did not. The town enacted strict building-height restrictions, keeping most structures to three stories or fewer. It resisted large hotel chains and resort-style development. This wasn’t an accident of geography or an inability to develop. It was a documented, repeated, community choice to stay small and preserve the Old Floridian character of the town rather than cash in on the tourism boom. That choice meant slower growth, less wealth concentration, and a different buyer profile than a boom-town beach resort. For an agent today, that choice, made decades ago, is the actual reason the town exists the way it does.

World War II and beyond: a stable, unglamorous growth pattern

World War II brought economic boost to Florida as it did elsewhere in the country, and Flagler Beach benefited. But it never became a major military hub or tourism juggernaut. Instead, it followed a pattern of steady, modest growth through the middle of the 20th century, more resembling the quiet growth of DeLand or other inland towns than the boom-and-bust cycles of coastal Miami or Daytona Beach. This stability, boring as it may have seemed at the time, became the town’s actual competitive advantage by the late 20th century, when buyers began seeking refuge from overdeveloped, congested beach towns and found Flagler Beach’s preserved character genuinely attractive.

City status: 1963

In 1963, Flagler Beach formally became a city rather than just a town, a technical distinction that reflected its growing sophistication and the need for more complex municipal governance. The timing is worth noting: this came after the major boom years, after the highway system that would tie Florida together was in place, and as the postwar suburban expansion was reshaping all of Florida. Becoming a city meant Flagler Beach was mature enough to run its own government without relying on county services entirely, but it was still small enough to run that government with a single mayor and a handful of council members.

Hurricane damage and pier reconstruction: preserving the 1928 legacy

The 1928 pier survived storms, hurricanes, and the changing shoreline for nearly a century, but in September 2022, Hurricanes Ian and Nicole struck within weeks of each other and caused damage that threatened the pier’s survival. Rather than demolish it and start fresh, which would have been cheaper, Flagler Beach chose to reconstruct the pier while preserving the original 1928 pilings and foundation section closest to shore. That section, about 100 feet of the original pier structure, remains intact from 1928 to the present day, a tangible connection across nearly a century of the town’s identity. For an agent, this decision tells the story about what Flagler Beach values: preservation, connection to history, and the town’s commitment to staying itself rather than erasing its past.

Flagler Beach today, by the numbers

Flagler Beach’s 2026 population stands at 5,771, up from 5,161 in the 2020 census, a growth rate of about 11.82 percent over the five-year period and 1.8 percent annually. That growth is steady and deliberate, not explosive. The median age of 59.3 years (59.4 for males, 59.2 for females) reflects a true retirement and near-retirement population, distinct even from other Florida beach towns. Seventy-two percent of residents age 15 and older are married, and 17 percent have children under 18, suggesting a mix of retired couples, empty nesters, and retirees without dependent children. The town is 90.2 percent White, 6.6 percent Hispanic, 2.3 percent Asian, and 0.9 percent multiracial, with essentially zero Black or Native American population, making it one of the least diverse communities in Flagler County. That demographic composition is neither good nor bad to state, it is simply the documented reality an agent should know when working in Flagler Beach. Median household income of $84,632 puts the town comfortably above the national average and well above many Florida markets, reflecting both a retiree population with accumulated wealth and a stable, older, owner-occupied housing stock. The poverty rate of 8.44 percent is notably low, suggesting real economic stability and relatively low housing insecurity. Of the 2,519 households, 66.8 percent are families and 33.2 percent are individuals living alone, a meaningful indicator that Flagler Beach attracts both couples and solo retirees, not primarily young families seeking school-district proximity or employment centers.

The Flagler Beach Historical Museum: preserving the town’s own story

In 1925, the same year Flagler Beach incorporated, residents began thinking about how to preserve the town’s history. That commitment to remembering itself has resulted in the Flagler Beach Historical Museum, located at 207 South Central Avenue, one block west of the pier. The museum houses a genuine collection spanning from pre-Columbian and Timucua artifacts to images and objects from the 1920s and World War II, including exhibits on the German U-boat activity off the Florida coast during the war. The museum also features local surfing legends, severe weather events, and more recent history. It houses the welcome center for the Flagler County tourism office and features a flag that rode the Space Shuttle Endeavour into space. For an agent, the existence of this museum and its continued operation reflects something real: a town that values its own story enough to curate and preserve it, and a population that chooses to spend time engaging with that history rather than erasing it for redevelopment.

Restaurants and the local food landscape

Flagler Beach’s restaurant scene reflects the town’s deliberate character and aging population. Rather than chain restaurants and fast-casual dining, the town features independent, locally-owned establishments that have built reputations over years. The Oceanside Beach Bar & Grill serves breakfast, lunch, and dinner daily with reliable, casual beachside food and views. The Funky Pelican sits directly on the 1928 pier, offering indoor and outdoor seating with unbeatable ocean views, serving American favorites and fresh seafood including standout items like shrimp and grits and Cajun mahi tacos. The Island Grille operates inside a restored historic Victorian home from the 1920s, offering fine dining in a charming, historically preserved space with ocean views. Tortugas Florida Kitchen & Bar, a two-story beachfront establishment, specializes in “Florribbean” flavors, dishes combining Caribbean and Florida cuisines, with customer favorites like coconut curry chicken, bacon-wrapped plantains, and blackened Gulf shrimp fondue. Other established local options include Flagler Fish Company, Break Awayz, Friends Cafe, The Anchor, The Net By George, and Crave’s Coastal Kitchen. For an agent, a walkable downtown with this kind of independent, established dining is a genuine lifestyle selling point, particularly for retirees and buyers choosing Flagler Beach specifically to escape chain-restaurant suburbia.

Things to do: beyond the pier and beach

While the beach and pier are Flagler Beach’s primary attractions, the town and surrounding area offer sustained activities that keep both residents and visitors engaged. The Flagler Beach Historical Museum, already mentioned, draws locals and tourists interested in the town’s past. Gamble Rogers Memorial State Recreation Area, a 145-acre state park at the south end of town, offers 68 beachside and riverside campsites, nature trails through maritime hammock forest, fishing, boat launching, picnicking, and birding, all for a $5 per-vehicle day-use fee or around $40 per night for camping. The A1A River to Sea Trail, winding along the coastal corridor with over 135 miles of connected paths, draws cyclists and pedestrians seeking scenic views and nature access. Water-sports options include kayak rentals and guided eco-tours of the Intracoastal Waterway, Flagler Surf School for surf lessons, and Marineland, a nearby marine-life facility where visitors can swim with dolphins. Equestrian Adventures of Florida offers beach horseback riding, a specific, memorable activity. Local parks offer birdwatching, and the nearby Florida Agricultural Museum preserves 1890s-pioneer homestead buildings, 1930s-citrus-farm structures, and a historic dairy barn. Downtown Flagler Beach hosts First Friday events monthly, bringing street musicians, craft vendors, and local artisans together. This range of activities isn’t overwhelming like a major tourism destination, but it is sustained and genuine, offering reasons for residents to have entertainment and engagement without ever leaving a small-town context.

A town that chose to stay itself

Flagler Beach’s history, from 1909 Ocean City Beach through 1925 incorporation through 2026 present day, is fundamentally the story of a town that was presented with choices and made the same choice repeatedly: to stay small, to preserve its character, and to resist the high-rise resort development and tourism-economy transformation that happened everywhere else on Florida’s coast. That choice was documented, deliberate, and contested at various points in time, not inevitable. But it stuck. A buyer looking at a cottage in Flagler Beach, or choosing the town for retirement, is responding to that choice, whether they know it or not. An agent who understands this history as real community decision-making, not just nostalgia or accident, has something genuine to offer a client: not just a listing description, but an explanation of why the town actually feels the way it does, and why someone would choose it deliberately over Daytona Beach or any other alternative.
