What's on the Florida Real Estate Exam? A Breakdown by Topic

Quick answer

The Florida real estate sales associate exam is a 100-question, multiple-choice test covering three broad areas: real estate math, national real estate principles and practices, and Florida-specific real estate law and license law. It’s administered on behalf of the DBPR, and you need to score at least 75% to pass. There’s no separate math-only or law-only pass/fail; it’s one combined score across the whole test.

Key takeaways
  • The exam has 100 multiple-choice questions and a passing score of 75%, set by the Florida DBPR.
  • Content splits across three broad areas: real estate math, national principles and practices, and Florida-specific law and statutes.

What's actually tested on the Florida real estate exam?

The Florida real estate sales associate exam is a state exam, administered on behalf of the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) through a testing vendor. It's closed-book and multiple-choice, and it draws from the same 63-hour pre-license course you completed to become eligible to sit for it. Broadly, the content falls into three buckets: real estate math, national real estate principles and practices, and Florida-specific real estate law. It's one combined exam and one combined score, not three separate pass/fail sections.

How many questions, and what score do you need?

The exam is 100 multiple-choice questions, and you need to score at least 75% to pass, a threshold set by the DBPR. Exact timing and question-by-question weighting are set by the state and can be adjusted, so rather than memorizing a precise count of how many questions come from each topic, plan to be reasonably strong across all three areas. A candidate who's excellent at math but shaky on Florida law can still fail the same exam as one who's excellent at law but weak on math.

Real estate math: the section that surprises people

Math questions cover things like proration of taxes and expenses at closing, commission splits and calculations, loan-to-value ratios, points, square footage and area problems, and appreciation or depreciation. The math itself (percentages, basic algebra) usually isn't the hard part. What trips people up is that it's presented as word problems, where you first have to figure out which numbers matter and which formula applies before you ever start calculating. Working through practice math problems repeatedly, not just reviewing formulas, is what actually builds speed here.

National real estate principles and practices

This is the largest general knowledge block, and it covers concepts that apply to real estate practice broadly, not just in Florida: types of property ownership, agency relationships and fiduciary duties, contracts, financing and mortgage basics, appraisal and valuation concepts, fair housing law, types of deeds, and land use or zoning. Much of this overlaps with what a licensed agent uses in day-to-day practice, so it tends to stick better once you see it applied to a real transaction, not just read as a definition.

Florida-specific law and statutes

This section is where Florida candidates can't lean on general real estate knowledge; it has to be learned specifically. It covers Florida license law under Chapter 475 of the Florida Statutes, the authority and rules of the Florida Real Estate Commission (FREC), brokerage relationship disclosure requirements specific to Florida, license status rules (active, inactive, and renewal requirements), and Florida-specific disclosure obligations in a transaction. Because it's largely memorization of rules rather than applied logic, it rewards repeated review more than any single study technique.

What if you don't pass on the first try?

A single missed section doesn't mean starting over. Candidates who don't clear the 75% threshold can retake the exam, and most people who fail the first time do so by a narrow margin in one of the three areas rather than across the board. That's actually useful information: a near-miss tells you exactly where to concentrate before your next attempt, instead of re-studying everything from scratch at the same intensity.

How to study so each area actually sticks

Most pre-license course providers offer practice exams that break your results down by category, which is the fastest way to see whether you're actually behind on math, national principles, or Florida law specifically, instead of guessing. A realistic plan: take a full practice exam early to find your weak area, spend extra repetition on real estate math since it's the most common stumbling block, treat Florida-specific statutes as a separate memorization pass rather than folding it into general review, and save your last few days before test day for timed, full-length practice runs rather than new material. The goal isn't perfection in every category; it's not having one genuinely weak spot that drags the combined score under 75%.

Once you've passed, the exam becomes the easy part to look back on, since the next stretch (choosing where to activate your license and finding real training) matters just as much. A new license is inactive until you place it with a sponsoring broker, and the brokerage you choose shapes how quickly the material you just studied turns into an actual first closing.
