When Does Your Florida Real Estate License Expire?

Home›Keep Your License Working›When It Expires

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Florida real estate licenses expire on either March 31 or September 30, on a two-year cycle. Which of those two dates applies to you depends on when you were originally licensed, so there is no single deadline shared by all Florida agents. If the date lands on a weekend or a holiday it extends to the next business day. The only reliable way to find your own expiration date is to look up your license record in the state licensing portal at MyFloridaLicense.com, and it is worth doing that rather than estimating, because the education you owe is different for a first renewal than for any renewal after it.

Key takeaways
  • There are only two possible expiration dates in Florida: March 31 and September 30. Yours is set by when you were first licensed.
  • The cycle is two years, so your date repeats on the same day and month every other year.
  • A date falling on a weekend or holiday extends to the next business day.
  • Florida statute requires the department to notify you 90 days before expiration, and that single notice goes to whatever contact information the state has on file for you.
  • Look the date up in the state licensing portal rather than relying on memory, a brokerage's records, or a course provider's reminder email.

There is no universal renewal day in Florida real estate. Agents licensed in the same office in the same year can carry different expiration dates, which is exactly why the question comes up so often and why secondhand answers are unreliable. Here is how the dates are assigned, how to find yours, and what to do with it once you have it.

Once you know your date, our overview of how to renew your Florida real estate license covers what to do with it.

The two dates, and only two

Florida real estate licenses expire on either March 31 or September 30. That is the entire set of possibilities. The cycle runs two years, so once you know which of the two applies to you, your date repeats on that same day every other year for the rest of your career, assuming your license stays active and in good standing.

If your expiration date falls on a weekend or a state holiday, it extends to the next business day. That is a small mercy and not a reason to plan around the edge of the deadline.

Why yours is different from someone else's

Your date is tied to when you were originally licensed. Florida assigns you to the renewal cycle that falls closest to two years from your initial licensure, which is why two agents who got licensed a few months apart can land on opposite dates. It is not chosen by your brokerage, it does not reset when you change brokers, and it does not follow the calendar year.

This is worth internalizing because it makes secondhand information genuinely dangerous here. A colleague telling you renewal is coming up in September is telling you about their license, not yours.

How to find your actual date

Look it up in the state licensing portal at MyFloridaLicense.com. Your license record shows your current status and your expiration date directly. This takes a few minutes and it removes every source of ambiguity at once.

While you are in there, confirm two other things on the same screen. First, your status, since active, voluntarily inactive and involuntarily inactive carry different obligations. Second, your contact information, for reasons covered below. If you are unclear on the status distinctions, our page comparing active and inactive Florida licenses lays them out.

The one notice you get, and how people miss it

Florida statute requires the department to give a licensee notice 90 days before a license expires. That is a genuine protection and it is also the whole of it. One notice, three months out, delivered to whatever address and email the state has on file.

The agents who miss renewal are rarely the ones who ignored a warning. They are the ones who never received it, because they moved, changed email providers, or were relying on a former brokerage's office address that stopped forwarding mail. Updating your contact information with the state is a five-minute task that protects the only automatic reminder in the entire system.

It follows that you should not treat the state notice as your primary reminder. Put your own date in your own calendar, with an alert far enough ahead to actually complete coursework, not a week before it closes.

How early can you renew?

You can begin the renewal process well before your expiration date, and there is no benefit to waiting. Renewing early does not shorten your next cycle or move your date forward. Your next expiration remains two years from the current one.

The practical reason to move early is coursework, not paperwork. Submitting a renewal takes minutes. Completing 14 hours of continuing education, or 45 hours of post-license education, does not. Agents who leave it to the final weeks are the ones who discover their course package was missing a required component with no time left to fix it.

What the date actually tells you

Knowing your expiration date matters less on its own than knowing which renewal it represents.

If this is your first renewal since initial licensure, the date is a hard deadline for post-license education: 45 hours for a sales associate, 60 for a broker. Missing it does not make your license inactive, it makes it null and void under Florida law. Our page on the 45-hour post-license course covers that requirement in full, and it is the single most consequential deadline in a Florida agent's career.

If this is any later renewal, the date is the deadline for 14 hours of continuing education, structured as 3 hours of core law, 3 hours of ethics and business practices, and 8 hours of specialty credit. Missing it makes the license involuntarily inactive, which is recoverable on a defined path.

Same date, very different stakes. If you are not certain which renewal you are facing, the portal will tell you, and it is worth confirming rather than assuming. The full renewal requirements page sets out both paths side by side.

Your date does not change when you change brokerages

This confuses people reasonably often. Transferring your license to a new broker updates your employing broker of record. It does not touch your expiration date, your renewal cycle, or your education requirement. A new brokerage does not reset your clock, and moving does not buy you time.

The one thing a move genuinely affects is who is paying attention. An agent in transition is an agent whose renewal reminder may be going to an old office. If you are switching close to your date, our page on whether to switch before or after your renewal works through the sequencing so nothing falls between the two offices.

Does anyone track this for you?

Legally, no. Your license is yours and the obligation is yours alone. In practice, brokerages vary enormously on this. Some run a compliance calendar and flag agents approaching renewal months in advance, particularly first renewals where the stakes are highest. Others have no idea when any of their agents expire.

That is a fair and specific question to ask any brokerage you are evaluating, and the answer tells you more than it seems to. An office that knows which of its agents are approaching a first renewal is an office with real administrative infrastructure behind its agents. It is a small thing that reveals a large one.
