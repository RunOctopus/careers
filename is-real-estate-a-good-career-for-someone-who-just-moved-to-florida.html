Is Real Estate a Good Career for Someone Who Just Moved to Florida?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Career Fit After Moving to Florida

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Yes, real estate can be a good career for someone who just moved to Florida, but it starts out harder than for a local-born agent. Most new agents get their earliest deals from their sphere of influence: family, old friends, former neighbors, people who already trust them. A newcomer doesn’t have that web built yet, so the first year usually leans more on brokerage-provided leads, mentorship, and deliberately building local ties (through an HOA, church, gym, or volunteer group) faster than a typical resident would need to.

The honest answer

Real estate can absolutely be a good career for someone who just moved to Florida, but it starts out harder than it does for an agent who grew up in the area. The reason isn’t licensing or market knowledge, both of those are learnable in months. The reason is that most new agents get their first handful of deals from people who already know and trust them: family, old neighbors, former coworkers, the friend from your kid’s soccer team you’ve known for a decade. That web of relationships is usually called a sphere of influence, and it’s the single biggest source of leads for a brand-new agent almost everywhere. If you just relocated, you don’t have that web here yet. You have to build it.

Why the sphere of influence matters so much early on

New agents rarely win business by being the most polished marketer or the fastest to answer the phone, not at first. They win it because someone already likes and trusts them enough to hand over the biggest financial transaction of their life. That trust takes time to build with strangers, but it already exists with people who’ve known you for years. A local-born agent can often close two or three deals in year one just from people they grew up around. An agent who moved to Florida six months ago doesn’t have that shortcut, so the first year usually leans more heavily on referrals from the brokerage, open houses, and cold outreach, which convert more slowly.

What actually works when you don’t have a local network yet

The gap is real, but it’s not a dead end. Newcomers who succeed tend to do two things differently. First, they choose a brokerage that actively feeds leads and mentorship to new agents rather than one that expects everyone to arrive with a built-in client list. A brokerage with an established local reputation, an existing referral pipeline, and agents willing to mentor closes a meaningful part of the gap simply by putting you in front of buyers and sellers you’d otherwise have no way to reach. Second, they treat the first year as a deliberate relationship-building project instead of waiting for a network to appear on its own.

Building local roots faster than usual

Since you can’t rely on decades of history in the community, the honest move is to build local ties on purpose and faster than a typical newcomer would. That means joining your HOA board or at least showing up to meetings, getting involved in a local church, gym, parents’ group, or hobby club, and showing up consistently rather than occasionally. It means introducing yourself to neighbors instead of waiting to be introduced. Volunteering with a local nonprofit or coaching a kids’ team puts you in regular contact with the same people over months, which is exactly how trust gets built. None of this replaces years of history, but it compresses the timeline considerably when you’re intentional about it.

Florida’s growth is a real tailwind

One thing working in a newcomer’s favor: Florida continues to draw a steady stream of people relocating from other states, and many of those transplants would rather work with an agent who understands what it’s like to move somewhere new than one who’s never left the area. If you recently went through the process of finding a home, a neighborhood, and a school district in Florida yourself, that firsthand experience is a genuine selling point with other newcomers, not a weakness. It won’t replace a sphere of influence overnight, but it gives you a story to tell that local-born agents can’t.

A realistic timeline

Most new agents, transplant or not, take somewhere in the range of a few months to a year before closing a steady flow of deals, and agents without an existing local network often sit toward the slower end of that range in year one. That’s not a reason to avoid the career, it’s a reason to go in with realistic expectations, some savings to cover the ramp-up period, and a brokerage that will actually help you generate business rather than leave you to figure it out alone. Income in real estate is commission-based and uneven by nature, and that unevenness is more pronounced for anyone starting without a built-in client base.

What to look for in a brokerage as a newcomer

If you just moved to Florida and are weighing this career, the brokerage decision matters more than it would for someone with deep local roots. Look for an established firm with a genuine local reputation, structured mentorship for new agents, and lead-sharing that doesn’t require you to have already built a network.
