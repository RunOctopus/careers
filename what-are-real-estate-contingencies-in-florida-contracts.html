What Are Real Estate Contingencies, and What Are the Common Types in Florida?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Real Estate Contingencies

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

A contingency is a condition written into a real estate contract that has to be satisfied for the deal to move forward as agreed. If the condition isn't met, and the contract was written correctly, the buyer typically has the right to cancel and get their earnest money deposit back rather than being forced to close or forfeit their deposit. The common contingencies in Florida contracts are the financing (or mortgage) contingency, the inspection contingency, the appraisal contingency, and, less often, a contingency tied to the sale of the buyer's current home. New agents need to be able to explain each one clearly, because a buyer who doesn't understand what their contract actually protects them from is a buyer who can get badly surprised, or badly burned, later.

Key takeaways
  • A contingency is a condition that must be satisfied for a contract to remain fully binding on both parties. If it fails, and the contract language supports it, the buyer can typically cancel and recover their earnest money deposit.
  • The financing (mortgage) contingency lets a buyer cancel if they can't secure loan approval on the terms specified in the contract, within the agreed timeframe.
  • The inspection contingency gives a buyer the right to have the property professionally inspected and to negotiate repairs, a price adjustment, or cancellation based on what's found.
  • The appraisal contingency protects a buyer if the lender's appraisal comes in below the contract price, since most lenders won't finance more than the appraised value.
  • A sale-of-buyer's-home contingency ties the purchase to the buyer successfully selling their current property first, and it's less common in competitive markets because sellers tend to view it as added risk.

What is a real estate contingency?

A contingency is a condition, spelled out directly in the purchase contract, that has to be satisfied for the contract to remain fully binding and for the sale to proceed as written. Contingencies exist because a real estate purchase isn't a single, instant transaction; it's a process that unfolds over days or weeks, involving financing, inspections, appraisals, and other steps that can't be fully confirmed the moment a contract is signed. A contingency builds in a specific, agreed-upon way for the buyer, and occasionally the seller, to exit the contract if one of those steps doesn't go as expected, without being penalized for it. The alternative, a contract with no contingencies at all, puts the buyer in a much more exposed position, since they're legally bound to close (or risk losing their deposit) regardless of what an inspection, appraisal, or their own financing turns up.

How contingencies protect a buyer's earnest money

Earnest money is the deposit a buyer puts down at contract signing to show they're serious about the purchase, and it's held, typically by a title company, broker, or attorney, until closing. If a buyer simply backs out of a deal for no contractual reason, they generally risk forfeiting that deposit to the seller as agreed damages. Contingencies change that calculation entirely: if a contingency isn't satisfied, financing falls through, an inspection turns up a major problem, an appraisal comes in low, and the buyer cancels within the terms and timeframes the contract actually specifies, the buyer is typically entitled to get their earnest money back rather than losing it. This is exactly why the specific wording and deadlines in a contingency clause matter so much. A contingency that isn't properly invoked, or one where the deadline has already passed, can leave a buyer without the protection they thought they had.

The financing (mortgage) contingency

The financing contingency, sometimes called a mortgage contingency, protects a buyer who's purchasing with a loan rather than cash. It gives the buyer a defined window to secure loan approval on the general terms described in the contract, and if that approval doesn't come through, despite the buyer making a good-faith effort to obtain it, the buyer can typically cancel and recover their deposit. This matters because loan approval isn't guaranteed just because a buyer was pre-qualified or pre-approved earlier in the process; a lender's underwriting can still turn up an issue, an appraisal can affect the loan amount, or a buyer's financial situation can change between pre-approval and the final commitment. Without this contingency, a buyer whose financing falls through late in the process could be on the hook to close anyway or lose their deposit.

The inspection contingency

The inspection contingency gives a buyer the right to have the property professionally inspected within a set period after contract signing, and to respond to what the inspection finds, by asking the seller to make repairs, negotiating a credit or price reduction, or, if the issues are serious enough and the contract terms allow it, canceling the contract and getting the deposit back. This is often the contingency buyers care about most directly, since it's the one most likely to surface something they genuinely didn't know about the property: an aging roof near the end of its life, evidence of past water intrusion, an HVAC system that's failing, or an electrical issue that wasn't visible on a walkthrough. A well-explained inspection contingency also sets realistic expectations: it's not a tool to renegotiate the price over every minor cosmetic issue, but it is real protection against discovering a significant, previously unknown problem after the ink is already dry.

The appraisal contingency

The appraisal contingency protects a buyer specifically against the risk that the home appraises for less than the agreed contract price. Since most mortgage lenders won't lend more than a property's appraised value, a low appraisal can leave a real gap between what the buyer agreed to pay and what the bank is actually willing to finance. With an appraisal contingency in place, if the home appraises below the contract price, the buyer typically has options: renegotiate the price with the seller, cover the difference themselves, or, if those paths don't work out, cancel the contract and recover their deposit rather than being forced to either come up with unexpected cash or lose their earnest money. Without this contingency, a low appraisal can put a buyer in a genuinely difficult financial position with very little recourse.

The sale-of-buyer's-home contingency

Less common, but still real, is a contingency tied to the buyer successfully selling their own current home first. This protects a buyer who needs the proceeds from their existing property to complete the new purchase, letting them cancel if their current home doesn't sell within an agreed timeframe. Sellers tend to view this contingency as added risk and uncertainty, since it ties their own closing to a separate transaction they have no control over, which is exactly why it's used less often in competitive markets or multiple-offer situations, where a seller can often choose an offer without this condition attached instead.

Why a new agent has to explain these clearly, especially to a first-time buyer

Contingencies are one of the areas where a knowledgeable agent adds real, tangible value to a first-time buyer, because the difference between a contract with the right contingencies, properly worded and with realistic deadlines, and one without them can be the difference between a buyer walking away from a bad situation with their deposit intact or losing real money over something that wasn't actually their fault. A new agent should be able to explain, in plain language, exactly what each contingency in a specific contract protects against, what the deadlines are, and what happens if one of them isn't met. Buyers who understand this going in are calmer and make better decisions when a real issue does come up mid-transaction, rather than panicking or missing a deadline that would have protected them.

What happens if contingency deadlines aren't met

Contingencies only protect a buyer if they're actually exercised correctly and on time. Every contingency in a Florida contract comes with a specific deadline: a certain number of days to complete an inspection, a certain number of days to obtain loan approval, a defined window tied to when the appraisal comes back. If a buyer wants to invoke a contingency and cancel, the notice generally has to be given within that window, in writing, following whatever process the contract specifies. Miss the deadline, and a buyer can lose the protection entirely, even if the underlying problem, a failed inspection, a denied loan, a low appraisal, is completely real. This is one of the most common and most avoidable mistakes in a real transaction, and it's exactly why a new agent needs to track these dates as carefully as the client does, sometimes more carefully, since a missed deadline can turn a buyer's real protection into nothing at all.

Contingencies as a negotiating tool, not just a protection

It's worth understanding that contingencies aren't only a shield for the buyer after the fact, they also function as a real negotiating factor before an offer is even accepted. In a competitive market, a seller comparing multiple offers will often view a contract with fewer contingencies, or shorter contingency deadlines, as a stronger, more certain offer, since it reduces the number of ways the deal could fall apart later. That's part of why some buyers choose to waive certain contingencies, most often the appraisal or financing contingency, in a competitive offer, understanding clearly that doing so trades away real protection in exchange for a more competitive position. A new agent needs to be able to explain this tradeoff honestly to a buyer client: waiving a contingency can help win a bidding war, but it also means giving up the right to walk away and recover earnest money if that specific issue comes up later, and that's a decision a buyer should make with full understanding of what they're actually giving up, not a box to check quickly to keep an offer competitive.
