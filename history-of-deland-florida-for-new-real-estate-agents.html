The History of DeLand, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of DeLand

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

DeLand started as a wilderness area called Persimmon Hollow before Henry A. DeLand, a wealthy baking-soda manufacturer from New York, bought land there in 1876 and set out to build a cultured, educated town he called the Athens of Florida. A brutal 1885 freeze wiped out the citrus fortune he'd staked the town's future on, and he handed his unfinished college over to a friend, hat maker John B. Stetson, who saved it and gave it his name. That founding story, not just the nickname, explains why DeLand still reads as a deliberate, historic, culturally serious town rather than a generic Florida suburb, and why buyers choose it on purpose.

Key takeaways
  • DeLand was originally known as Persimmon Hollow before Henry A. DeLand purchased land there in 1876 and platted the townsite that December.
  • Henry DeLand made his fortune in baking soda, not real estate or citrus, and used it to fund the town's early streets, schools, and churches.
  • The devastating freeze of 1885 destroyed DeLand's citrus groves and his personal fortune, forcing him to hand his young academy's endowment to John B. Stetson, inventor of the cowboy hat.
  • Stetson's rescue is why the university carries his name instead of DeLand's, even though DeLand founded the town and the school.
  • The 1884 railroad, the 1921 Athens Theatre, and a 1985 downtown revival each mark a real turning point in how the town grew, and each still shapes what an agent is actually selling today.

Before any of it: the Timucua

Long before Henry DeLand ever saw the area, the St. Johns River Valley, the corridor that runs through modern DeLand, Sanford, Astor, Palatka, and Jacksonville, was Timucua territory, home to the Mocama-speaking peoples of that river system for centuries. Spanish explorer Alvaro Mexia documented Timucua settlements in the region as early as 1605, and physical evidence of that habitation still survives in Volusia County today, most visibly in the large shell middens preserved at Tomoka State Park north of Daytona Beach. The Timucua did not survive as a distinct people past the colonial era, decimated by disease and conflict following European contact. No development, no listing, and no modern neighborhood in DeLand changes that this land had a long human history before 1876. An agent doesn't need to become a historian of it, but pretending the area's story starts with a New York businessman buying land is simply inaccurate.

Before DeLand: Persimmon Hollow

The land that became DeLand had no grand Anglo-American name before 1876. Settlers called the area Persimmon Hollow, a modest wilderness pocket of central Florida reached mostly by steamboat up the St. Johns River from Jacksonville and Sanford, since the railroads that would later define the region hadn't yet arrived. That detail matters for an agent today: DeLand wasn't destined for anything in particular. It became what it is because one specific person decided to build something deliberate here, and that decision still shapes the town's character more than a century later.

A baking-soda fortune builds a town on purpose

Henry Addison DeLand wasn't a Florida land speculator. He'd spent 22 years running the DeLand Chemical Company in New York, a baking-soda business, before a vacation trip introduced him to Persimmon Hollow. He liked what he saw enough to buy a large tract of land, and instead of just platting lots for profit, he set out to build something specific: a citrus, agricultural, and tourism center with real intellectual and moral ambition behind it, not just another rail-line town. He personally advanced the money for streets, a school, and churches. He platted the townsite on December 6, 1876, and marketed it to settlers on the promise of cultured, intellectual community, not just cheap land. That's the origin of the “Athens of Florida” identity that still shows up in how the town thinks of itself.

The freeze that changed everything

DeLand staked much of his personal wealth on the citrus industry he was building alongside the town, and in 1883 he founded DeLand Academy as the educational anchor of his vision. Then, in 1885, a severe freeze destroyed the region's orange groves, DeLand's fortune included. Unable to keep funding the academy's endowment, DeLand turned to a friend for help: John B. Stetson, the inventor of the cowboy hat and one of the wealthiest manufacturers in America. Stetson took over the endowment, saved the school, and made DeLand his winter home for the rest of his life. The school was incorporated as DeLand University in 1887, then renamed John B. Stetson University in 1889 in his honor, and shortened to simply Stetson University in the 1990s, which is why the school carries Stetson's name today rather than DeLand's, even though DeLand founded both the town and the original academy. It's a real, human story of a fortune lost and a town saved by someone else's money, not a footnote.

Stetson grows into a genuine institution, not just a rescued academy

Stetson didn't stay a small rescued school. In 1900, it opened Florida's first law school, Stetson University College of Law, under founding dean Albert J. Farrah, admitting five students that first fall. In 1905, Stetson's law school became the first in Florida to admit women, and in 1908 the state's first female lawyer graduated from it. That law school operated in DeLand for over five decades before a postwar enrollment boom pushed it to relocate in 1954 to a new campus in Gulfport, on Florida's west coast, where it remains today, so an agent should know Stetson's law program is no longer physically in DeLand even though its history began there. Back in DeLand, Stetson kept building genuine institutions of its own: its Model United States Senate, founded in 1971, is the oldest collegiate-level model Senate in the country and still runs today. None of this is trivia to memorize. It's the difference between telling a buyer “there's a university here” and being able to explain why that university has real, sustained academic weight behind it.

The railroad arrives, and DeLand stops depending on the river

Until the 1880s, reaching DeLand meant traveling by steamboat. That changed when the Orange Ridge, DeLand and Atlantic Railroad was incorporated in 1881 and began operating on July 28, 1884, a narrow-gauge line connecting DeLand Landing to a depot downtown. The railroad tied DeLand into Florida's broader rail network for the first time, and the modest population and lumber-and-citrus economic growth that followed set the pattern for the next several decades: a real, if unspectacular, inland town growing steadily rather than booming and busting with a single industry.

The 1920s building boom, the Athens Theatre, and a courthouse built to impress

Like much of Florida, DeLand saw real building activity in the 1920s land-boom years. The Athens Theatre, an Italian Renaissance building designed by Orlando architect Murray S. King, opened January 6, 1922, and remains one of the best-preserved classic American theatres in the South today. Just as telling: starting in 1927, Volusia County built a new courthouse in downtown DeLand in the Neoclassical style, completed in 1929 at a cost of $500,000, a serious sum at the time, featuring fluted Corinthian columns, marble staircases, a copper-clad dome, and a stained-glass interior cupola. It was, by contemporary description, one of the most beautiful county administration buildings in the South, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1987 as part of the Downtown DeLand Historic District. Downtown filled in with the Mediterranean Revival buildings that still define its architectural character today. This is the physical building stock an agent is actually showing when they walk a buyer through downtown: not a stage set, but real 1920s construction from a specific, documented boom period, and a courthouse the county deliberately built to be impressive, not just functional.

The Depression, the war, and a slow, steady town

DeLand wasn't immune to the Great Depression. Times were hard, businesses struggled, and growth slowed. World War II brought a real economic boost back to the town, as it did to much of the country, and DeLand continued its pattern of steady, unspectacular growth through the mid-20th century rather than the explosive boom-and-bust cycles some coastal Florida towns saw.

From WWII dive-bombers to the Skydiving Capital of the World

DeLand's municipal airport has a genuinely remarkable second life most newcomers never expect. From 1942 to 1946, it operated as Naval Air Station DeLand, a WWII dive-bomber training base. Just over a decade after the war, on December 26, 1959, Dr. John Gaffney led the Central Florida Sky Divers Club to jump from a Tri-Pacer at that same airport, and skydiving there has run continuously ever since, making it the longest continuously operating skydiving site in the world. Skydive DeLand, the commercial operation most visitors know today, was founded in 1982, and DeLand has hosted state, national, and world record formation jumps since the first 72-person formation in 1983, right up through a 2022 world record set by eight skydivers all over age 80. DeLand genuinely markets itself as the Skydiving Capital of the World, and it's earned. For an agent, this isn't just a fun fact: it's a real, ongoing driver of visitors, seasonal residents, and a specific enthusiast community that returns to DeLand again and again, some of whom eventually buy. It also means an agent should know which neighborhoods sit under the jump zone and understand what that means for buyers sensitive to aircraft and parachute traffic.

DeLand and the civil rights era

DeLand's history includes the same Jim Crow-era segregation that shaped every Florida town in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and it's part of the real record, not something to skip past. DeLand had a segregated Black high school, Euclid High School, and like most of the state, its public schools, businesses, and facilities were legally segregated until well into the 1960s. Real, documented change came from real people: in 1951, years before the U.S. Supreme Court's 1954 Brown v. Board of Education decision, Stetson University's own faculty went on record favoring desegregation, a genuinely early stance for a Southern institution. In 1960, African American college students, organizing in the same wave of sit-ins that swept the South that year, held a sit-in at a segregated lunch counter in downtown DeLand, part of the same movement documented at lunch counters across Florida and the country. Meaningful school integration in Florida took years longer to actually achieve after the legal rulings, DeLand included. This history matters for the same reason the rest of this page does: some buyers and sellers, particularly longtime DeLand families, carry it directly, and an agent who understands it as real local history, not an uncomfortable footnote, is better equipped for honest conversations about the community they're serving.

The decline before the revival, and choosing preservation on purpose

Downtown DeLand's preservation story isn't a straight line. Through the 1980s and into the 1990s, real decline set in: a number of older downtown buildings were actually demolished to make way for newer residential growth, the opposite of preservation. The turning point was a deliberate choice, not an accident. In 1985, DeLand became one of the first five cities in Florida to earn Main Street designation, kicking off an organized, sustained preservation and revitalization effort run through MainStreet DeLand, the DeLand Historic Preservation Board, and the county's own preservation board. It worked: the Downtown DeLand Historic District was formally designated on December 23, 1987, protecting 68 historic downtown buildings, and by 1997 the effort had earned DeLand a national Great American Main Street Award from the National Trust for Historic Preservation, one of the country's top honors for exactly this kind of downtown revival. More than 600 DeLand properties in total now carry some form of historic-register recognition. None of that was inevitable. It was organizations and people choosing, within living memory, to save buildings that were actively being lost.

DeLand today: growth resumes after a century of steady, not spectacular, change

For most of its history, DeLand grew slowly and deliberately rather than booming. That's changed in recent years. The city's population has grown meaningfully since 2020, a real acceleration after more than a century of modest, steady expansion. An agent working DeLand today is arriving at a genuine inflection point: a town with 150 years of intentional, documented character that is now absorbing real growth for the first time at this pace. That combination, deep history plus real current demand, is a specific and useful thing to understand, not just a talking point.

DeLand today, by the numbers

DeLand's roughly 39-year median age (37 for men, just under 43 for women) puts it squarely between a retiree town and a young family suburb, consistent with a real college-town population mixed into a broader, older Volusia County base. Median household income runs around $71,800. The population is about 65% White, 12% Black, and 17% Hispanic or Latino, with smaller Asian and multiracial populations rounding it out, a genuinely more mixed demographic picture than the postcard version of “small Florida college town” suggests. None of this is trivia for an agent: it shapes what price points, school priorities, and neighborhood types actually move in DeLand, as distinct from the county-wide averages.

Who's actually from DeLand

Two Major League Baseball stars were born in DeLand, though neither is exactly a DeLand hometown story in the simple sense. Hall of Famer Chipper Jones, the Atlanta Braves legend and 1999 National League MVP, was born in DeLand but grew up in nearby Pierson, where his father coached at T. DeWitt Taylor High School. Cy Young winner Jacob deGrom was also born in DeLand, but was raised in DeLeon Springs just north of town and played his high school ball in Ormond Beach. Both are genuinely, verifiably tied to DeLand by birth, and it's a real, honest talking point, but an agent should get the nuance right rather than claim either as having grown up in DeLand itself, since neither did.

Where DeLand actually eats

Downtown DeLand's restaurant scene is a real, walkable draw in its own right, not just a backdrop to the historic architecture. Shady Oak Restaurant, an old-Florida seafood spot on the St. Johns River, has been serving the area since 1954. Downtown itself has filled in with genuine variety: Forno Bello and Tony's for two different takes on Italian, De La Vega Restaurante for family-owned Latin fusion, and Oudom Thai and Sushi, popular enough to regularly draw a line. For an agent, a walkable downtown with real, established (not chain) dining is a genuine lifestyle selling point for buyers who could choose a beach town instead and are weighing what daily life actually looks like.

What there actually is to do in DeLand

Beyond the historic downtown itself, at Stetson University the Gillespie Museum houses part of a roughly 15,000-specimen mineral and earth-science collection, and the Museum of Art - DeLand adds a second real cultural anchor downtown. History-minded visitors can tour the restored Stetson Mansion. A short drive out of town, De Leon Springs and Blue Spring State Parks (the latter technically in neighboring Orange City, but genuinely part of the area's identity) offer spring-fed swimming, kayaking, and, at Blue Spring, one of Florida's best manatee-watching spots in winter. Layer in the skydiving already covered above, and DeLand offers a real, varied answer to “what is there actually to do here,” not just a historic downtown to look at once.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer looking at a 1920s Garden District bungalow, or a family choosing DeLand over a beach town for its downtown and schools, is responding to decisions made by Henry DeLand, John B. Stetson, and a 1985 city council, whether they know those names or not. An agent who can explain why the architecture looks the way it does, why the university carries a different name than the town's founder, and why the historic district exists on purpose rather than by accident is offering something a generic market summary never can: a real answer to “why does this town feel this way?” That's a genuine, durable edge with buyers who are choosing DeLand deliberately, which, as the town's own founding story suggests, most of them are.
