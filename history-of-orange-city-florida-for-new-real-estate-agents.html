The History of Orange City, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Orange City

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Orange City grew up around Blue Spring, a first-magnitude spring that a Bartram called an “admirable fountain” in 1774, decades before any town existed. Gold-rush prospector Louis Thursby bought the spring in 1856 and turned it into Blue Spring Landing, a steamboat port shipping citrus and tourists up the St. Johns River. The town itself was founded in 1875 when seven investors bought roughly 5,000 acres of state land, and it was incorporated in 1882 and named for the orange groves surrounding it, groves the Great Freeze of 1894 to 1895 then wiped out almost entirely. What survived is what still shapes the town today: a hotel built in 1876 that’s the oldest in Volusia County, a historic district of more than 200 buildings, and Blue Spring itself, now a state park and a manatee refuge that regularly draws hundreds of manatees each winter. For an agent, that combination, real founding-era history plus a genuine natural landmark, is a durable, specific reason certain buyers choose Orange City on purpose.

Key takeaways
  • Louis Thursby, a former gold-rush prospector, bought Blue Spring in 1856 and built Blue Spring Landing, a working steamboat port that moved citrus and tourists on the St. Johns River decades before Orange City existed as a town.
  • Seven investors, led by Dr. Seth French, purchased about 5,000 acres of state land in 1875 and founded the town; it incorporated in 1882 and was named for the orange groves that covered the area.
  • The Great Freeze of 1894 to 1895 destroyed the citrus industry that gave the town its name, a real irony that shaped Orange City's economy for decades afterward.
  • The hotel the DeYarman family began building in 1875 still stands today as the 1876 Heritage Inn, the oldest operating hotel in Volusia County, though it was damaged in a 2026 vehicle crash and an agent should confirm its current status before referencing it.
  • Blue Spring became a state park in 1972 specifically to protect its winter manatee population, and it now regularly hosts several hundred manatees each winter, a real, ongoing draw distinct from anything else in West Volusia.
  • Orange City's Historic District, more than 200 buildings strong, was formally listed on the National Register in 2004, protecting the physical record of the town's 1875 to 1920s founding era.

Before any of it: what the Thursby House was built on

Long before Orange City existed as a town, the land around Blue Spring and the St. Johns River was Timucua territory, part of the same Mocama-speaking river-valley population that lived throughout this stretch of central Florida for centuries. That history isn't abstract here. When the Thursby family built their home on the bank of Blue Spring run in 1872, they built it directly on top of a large Indigenous shell midden, a mound of discarded shells and everyday debris left by generations of people who lived at that exact spot long before any settler arrived. The midden and the house that sits on it are both still there today, inside Blue Spring State Park. An agent doesn't need to become an archaeologist to sell Orange City, but it's worth knowing that the property everyone visits for manatees and kayaking was inhabited land first, not empty wilderness waiting to be found.

Bartram's “admirable fountain”

The first written description of Blue Spring by a European visitor comes from 1774, when botanist John Bartram paddled up the spring run and, according to the park's own historical marker, called it an “admirable fountain,” though he found the water's taste disagreeable. (Some Bartram scholars attribute the original account to his son William, who explored the same stretch of the St. Johns eight years earlier, in 1766; either way, a Bartram documented the spring decades before any town stood nearby.) What both Bartrams were looking at is still measurable today: Blue Spring is a first-magnitude spring, discharging roughly 100 million gallons of water a day into the St. Johns River, one of the largest single springs in the state. That's not a historical curiosity, it's the same physical feature an agent is selling proximity to right now.

Louis Thursby and Blue Spring Landing

The person who actually put Blue Spring to work was Louis Thursby, a former Gold Rush prospector who'd turned to orange growing. He bought Blue Spring in 1856 and moved his family there, building one of the first steamboat landings and one of the earliest orange groves on the upper St. Johns River. Blue Spring Landing became a genuinely busy port, decades before Orange City had a name: steamboats carried tourists and freight between the landing and Jacksonville and beyond, since there was no other practical way to move people or goods through this part of Florida yet. The Thursby family's 1872 house, built from center-cut pine milled in Savannah, Georgia, and shipped in by boat, still stands on the property today and operates as a house museum inside Blue Spring State Park, open Wednesday through Sunday. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2000. When the railroad reached the area in the 1880s, steamboat traffic on the St. Johns largely stopped, but Thursby's landing is the reason this specific spot on the river had any economic life at all before the town of Orange City existed.

Founding a town on purchased state land

Orange City itself dates to 1875, when seven men, Dr. Seth French, David P. Graves, John E. Stillman, William Hawley, John C. Thorpe, James M. Smith, and Allen Cameron, purchased roughly 5,000 acres of Florida's internal-improvement land, tracts the state sold off cheaply to encourage exactly this kind of settlement, and set out to build a town. Seth French emerged as the group's most prominent figure: he'd go on to serve in the Florida Senate in 1879 and later as the state's Commissioner of Immigration, actively recruiting new settlers to Florida. The house he built in 1876 still stands at 319 East French Avenue, was added to the National Register in 2003, and remains in his family, currently owned by his great-granddaughter. That's a genuinely direct line, one family, one house, 150 years, running straight through the middle of Orange City's founding story.

The hotel that's still (mostly) standing

The same summer the founders were platting the town, Hugh and Sophia DeYarman began building Orange City's first hotel, and the building they started is still there today at 300 South Volusia Avenue, now known as the 1876 Heritage Inn. For most of its history it's been described as the oldest operating hotel in Volusia County: it closed briefly in 1931 during the Depression, reopened in 1936 under new owner Ralph Hankinson, a race promoter, and kept running for the better part of a century after that as a working hotel, restaurant, and later an extended-stay property. In February 2026, a truck crash in the parking lot severely damaged the building's power, water, and fire-suppression systems, forcing dozens of extended-stay residents to relocate and the city to declare the structure temporarily unsafe. Repairs were underway by April 2026, with the owner stating she hoped to reopen within a couple of months, but as of this writing there's no confirmed, current reopening date. An agent referencing the Heritage Inn to a client should verify its operating status directly rather than assume it, but the building itself, and the 1875 founding story it represents, isn't going anywhere.

A name from the groves, made official

Orange City incorporated as a city on August 26, 1882, and the name wasn't marketing, it was literal: thousands of acres of orange groves covered the area in and around the new town, and the founders named it accordingly. The town's first public school organized in 1876, run by Major George J. Alden, the area's superintendent of schools, and growth through the 1880s tracked the citrus economy that gave the town its identity. For a few decades, Orange City was, in the most direct sense possible, a citrus town that happened to have a town's name to match.

The freeze that undid the town's own name

Twelve years after incorporation, in December 1894 and again in February 1895, a pair of catastrophic freezes hit Florida statewide, the worst the citrus industry had ever seen. Statewide, production collapsed from more than six million boxes of fruit a year to essentially none; growers across the state reported tens of thousands of acres planted in citrus and not a single box produced that season. Orange City's own namesake groves were part of that devastation, an outcome that's more than a little ironic for a town that had chosen its name specifically because of the orange trees surrounding it. The freeze pushed Florida's citrus belt permanently south toward the Indian River and Palm Beach regions, farther from the cold, and Orange City's economy had to rebuild around something other than the industry it was named for. It's a real, documented parallel to what happened to DeLand's citrus fortune in the freeze of 1885, just nine years earlier and one county over, and it's a reminder that Volusia County's founding-era towns were genuinely built and rebuilt around agriculture, not real estate speculation.

The water that impressed Rockefeller

Citrus wasn't the only thing Orange City had going for it. A mineral spring discovered in 1891 supplied bottled water that went on to win the Silver Medal, described at the time as the highest award the world could give for water purity, at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, formally the Louisiana Purchase Exposition. The water's reputation reportedly reached John D. Rockefeller Sr., who had Orange City Mineral Spring water shipped to him wherever he traveled and used it for bathing. It's a small, specific, verifiable fact, not a folk legend, and it says something real about how the town thought of its own natural resources even before Blue Spring itself became the main attraction: this was a place people associated with clean, valuable water long before anyone thought about manatees.

From citrus port to manatee refuge

Blue Spring's modern identity as a manatee sanctuary is recent, not ancient, and it's worth an agent knowing exactly how recent. In 1970, researchers tracked only 14 manatees using the spring run in a given winter. In 1971, an episode of Jacques Cousteau's Underwater World, filmed at Blue Spring and titled “The Forgotten Mermaids,” brought national attention to the manatees wintering there and helped convince the state to act. In 1972, the state purchased Blue Spring specifically to protect it as a manatee refuge, and it opened as Blue Spring State Park. The conservation effort worked, dramatically: wintering manatee counts passed 200 by 2005, hit a then-record 485 in 2018, and during a cold snap in February 2026 the park recorded an all-time high of 932 manatees in a single count, smashing the previous record of 736 set just weeks earlier on New Year's Day. Manatees gather at Blue Spring every winter, mid-November through March, because the spring holds a constant 72 degrees when the St. Johns River itself turns too cold for them to survive. This isn't a static piece of history. It's a real, currently accelerating natural phenomenon happening at the edge of town, and it's a genuinely different kind of draw than anything DeLand, Flagler Beach, or Port Orange can offer.

Orange City's Historic District: what's actually protected today

The physical record of Orange City's founding era survives in an unusually intact way. The Orange City Historic District, roughly bounded by Banana, Carpenter, French, and Orange Avenues, was added to the National Register of Historic Places on April 6, 2004, and it protects 211 historic buildings and three structures, a genuinely large district for a town this size. The Seth French House and the 1876 Heritage Inn both sit inside it, alongside homes like the Albertus Cottage and buildings like the First Congregational United Church of Christ. An agent showing a buyer a home inside this district is showing them something with real, documented legal protection behind its historic character, not just an old-looking street.

Two men who ended up in Orange City, and stayed

Orange City isn't a hometown-hero story in the usual sense, but it drew at least two genuinely notable people who chose to spend their final years there. Gaylord DuBois, one of the most prolific comic book writers in American history, wrote well over 300 Tarzan stories for Dell and Gold Key Comics between the late 1940s and early 1970s, along with thousands of other comics scripts across his career; he died in Orange City in 1993. Gar Samuelson, the original drummer for the thrash metal band Megadeth from 1984 to 1987, died in Orange City in 1999. Neither grew up in the town, and an agent shouldn't overstate the connection, but both are real, verifiable, and reflect the same quiet, low-key character that draws retirees and people looking to step out of a louder life today.

Orange City today, by the numbers

Orange City's population has grown meaningfully in recent years, from 12,632 at the 2020 census to an estimated 15,900 in 2026, roughly 25 percent growth in five years. The median age is 44.2 (39.7 for men, 47.1 for women), and median household income runs around $57,800, with a poverty rate near 10.4 percent. The population is about 66 percent White, 19 percent Hispanic or Latino, and 6 percent Black, with smaller Asian and multiracial populations rounding it out. Those numbers put Orange City meaningfully younger and less affluent, on paper, than a retiree-heavy beach town like Flagler Beach, but the presence of a genuine continuing-care retirement community, John Knox Village of Central Florida, which broke ground in 1972 and now houses more than 800 residents on its campus, shows the retiree draw is real even if it doesn't dominate the town-wide averages the way it does elsewhere in Volusia County.

Where Orange City eats

Orange City's dining scene is small but genuine, not chain-dominated. Blue Springs Brewing, operating as Riverwalk Pizzeria & Brew Pub on South Volusia Avenue, pairs an in-house craft brewery with scratch-made pizza, a real local gathering spot rather than a tourist stop. Orange Social, a scratch kitchen on Enterprise Road, runs a full bar and a menu built around fresher, less processed options than the typical roadside diner. Fire on the Bayou, on Town Center Drive, serves New Orleans-style Cajun and Creole food, gumbo, jambalaya, crawfish etouffee, in a casual, family-friendly setting. None of these are large operations, but they're the kind of independently run, specific places that give an agent something real to point to when a buyer asks what there actually is to do besides drive to DeLand or Sanford.

What there actually is to do in Orange City

Blue Spring State Park is the obvious center of gravity: swimming, kayaking, scuba diving, camping, and the Pine Island Trail, a 7.3-mile hiking route through the park, sit alongside the winter manatee viewing that draws visitors from well outside Volusia County. The Thursby House museum, inside the park, is open Wednesday through Sunday and tells the family's steamboat-era story directly. Valentine Park, a 20-acre park established in 1980 at the entrance to Blue Spring, adds sand volleyball, tennis and basketball courts, walking trails, and playgrounds for residents who aren't there specifically for the spring. The Spring to Spring Trail, a 26-mile multi-use trail network connecting DeBary, Orange City, DeLand, and DeLeon Springs State Park, runs a dedicated trailhead right off Blue Spring itself, giving cyclists and walkers a genuinely long, connected route through West Volusia rather than an isolated local loop. Family Fun Town rounds things out with go-karts, mini-golf, batting cages, and an arcade for families who want something other than nature and history. It's not a large list, but it's a real one, and most of it centers on the same natural feature that built the town in the first place.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer choosing Orange City over DeLand or Deltona is often choosing it, whether they say so or not, because of Blue Spring: the retiree drawn to a quieter, water-adjacent town; the nature-focused buyer who wants kayaking and manatees within a five-minute drive instead of a weekend trip; the small-business owner eyeing a tourism-adjacent property near a park that reliably draws visitors every winter. An agent who can explain that Blue Spring's manatee refuge is a specific, dated, still-growing conservation success (14 manatees in 1970, 932 in 2026), not just a postcard fact, and who knows that the historic district downtown is a legally protected 211-building record of an 1875 founding, not just old-looking streets, is offering something a generic listing sheet never can. That's the same kind of edge DeLand and Flagler Beach agents get from their own founding stories, applied to what actually makes Orange City different: a spring, not a beach, and a manatee season instead of a tourist season.
