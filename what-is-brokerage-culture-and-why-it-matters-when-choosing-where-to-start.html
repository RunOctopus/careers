What Is Brokerage Culture and Why It Matters When Choosing Where to Start

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Brokerage Culture

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Brokerage culture is how agents at an office actually treat each other day to day: collaborative or cutthroat, whether management is visible and accessible, and whether new agents get real mentorship or are left to figure things out alone. It matters as much as commission split, because a supportive office can help a new agent survive and grow through a difficult first year, while a cutthroat one can leave a promising agent stalled or gone within months. The best way to evaluate it is to visit the office in person and talk directly with agents who already work there, not just read the recruiting pitch.

  • Brokerage culture is how agents actually treat each other day to day, not what a recruiting pitch promises.

What does “brokerage culture” actually mean?

Every brokerage will tell you it has a great culture. What that phrase actually describes is simpler than the marketing makes it sound: how agents treat each other day to day, how much real support exists beyond the recruiting pitch, and whether the office feels like a place people want to be. Two brokerages can offer nearly identical commission splits and still produce completely different experiences for a new agent, because a split is a number and culture is everything that happens around that number.

Collaborative vs. cutthroat: the difference you feel in week one

Some offices run collaborative: agents share leads when they’re overloaded, answer each other’s questions in the hallway, and treat a colleague’s closed deal as a win for the office rather than a loss for themselves. Other offices run cutthroat: agents guard information, view every other agent as a competitor for the same buyers, and offer little beyond a desk and a login. Neither model is unusual, but the difference matters enormously for a brand-new agent who doesn’t yet have the confidence or client base to go it alone.

How visible and accessible is management?

A broker or manager who is genuinely reachable, someone you can flag down with a contract question or a nervous first-showing question, changes what your first year feels like. A manager who’s rarely in the office, or who’s competing for the same listings you are, sends a different signal: you’re mostly on your own. Ask directly how often the broker or manager is physically present, and whether they take their own listings. A non-competing manager has no incentive to keep the best leads for themselves.

Do new agents get real mentorship, or are they left alone?

Mentorship is one of the most overused words in recruiting conversations. The real question is what it looks like in practice during your first 90 days. Is there a structured onboarding, or a folder of PDFs and a “good luck”? Will an experienced agent walk through your first contract with you, or are you reading the state forms cold the night before a signing? New agents who get real, hands-on guidance through their first few transactions tend to make fewer costly mistakes and stick with the business longer than those who are handed a login and left to figure it out.

How to actually evaluate a brokerage’s culture before you join

You can’t evaluate culture from a recruiting slide deck or a website. It shows up in the office itself. Visit in person, ideally more than once and at different times of day, and pay attention to what you see: are agents talking to each other, or is everyone heads-down and isolated? Ask to speak with two or three current agents without a manager in the room, and ask them plainly what they’d change about the office. Ask a newer agent specifically what their first 90 days looked like. The answers you get one-on-one, away from the pitch, tell you far more than anything in a printed brochure.

Questions worth asking directly

Beyond a general office visit, a few pointed questions tend to surface culture fast: What happens when I have a question on my first contract, who do I actually call? How often does the broker meet with new agents one-on-one? Has an agent left recently, and if so, why? A brokerage confident in its own culture will answer these plainly. One that gets defensive or vague is telling you something too.

Red flags worth taking seriously

Watch for a few recurring warning signs: high turnover that no one wants to explain, a manager who’s never available because they’re busy producing their own deals, agents who seem guarded or unwilling to talk openly, or a recruiting pitch that’s heavy on splits and light on any mention of support. None of these is a dealbreaker in isolation, but together they paint a picture of an office built for agents who already know what they’re doing, not for someone starting out.

Why this decision matters more than the commission split

A slightly higher split at an office where you’re left alone can end up costing you far more than a slightly lower split at an office that helps you close your first several deals with confidence. Culture affects how quickly you learn, how many mistakes you avoid, and whether you stay in the business long enough to build the referral base that eventually makes the split matter less anyway.
