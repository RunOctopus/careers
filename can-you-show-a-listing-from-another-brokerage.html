Can You Show a Listing From Another Brokerage?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Showing Other Firms' Listings

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Yes, and doing so is most of the job. New agents sometimes assume they can only show houses their own company has listed, which would make representing a buyer nearly impossible, since any given firm holds a small fraction of what is for sale. The multiple listing service exists precisely so member brokerages cooperate: one firm lists a property and invites the others to bring buyers to it. What does need attention before you go is your own paperwork with the buyer and how you will be paid, both of which are settled in advance rather than assumed.

Key takeaways
  • You can show any property in the MLS, whoever listed it. Cooperation is the entire point of the system.
  • Your firm lists a fraction of the market, so a buyer's agent works almost entirely with other firms' listings.
  • The listing agent represents the seller. You are not working for them and should not act as though you are.
  • Get your representation and compensation arrangements agreed in writing before you start showing, not afterwards.
  • For-sale-by-owner and some new construction sit outside the MLS and need a separate conversation first.

This is one of those questions new agents feel silly asking and which nobody explains, because to everyone already in the business the answer is too obvious to say out loud.

Yes, and it is most of what a buyer's agent does

Think about the arithmetic. Any individual brokerage, however large locally, has listed a small share of the properties currently for sale in a two-county market. If an agent could only show their own firm's inventory, representing a buyer would be close to useless, because you would be showing them a fraction of what is available and ignoring the rest.

The system is built the other way round. The multiple listing service is a cooperative arrangement between member brokerages: a firm lists a property, publishes it to the other members, and invites them to bring buyers. That cooperation is the entire reason the MLS exists, and it is why access to it matters so much when you get licensed, as set out in which MLS and Realtor association you join here and whether you need to join the board and MLS to work as an agent.

So the honest framing is not that you are permitted to show another firm's listing. It is that showing other firms' listings is the ordinary condition of buyer representation.

How it actually works on a Tuesday

You find the property in the MLS. The listing includes the showing instructions, which vary: an appointment booked through a showing system, a call to the listing office, a lockbox with an access code, a requirement to give a certain amount of notice, or restrictions on times because the property is tenanted or the seller works shifts.

You follow those instructions, you get access, and you show it. Getting in physically is its own small subject, covered in how you get an MLS lockbox key in Volusia and Flagler County, and what to do once inside is in what you actually do at your first showing.

Two courtesies matter more than they sound, because this market is small and the same agents recur. Turn up when you said you would, or tell them you are running late. And leave the property exactly as you found it: lights off if they were off, doors locked, lockbox properly closed. Being the agent whose showings never produce a complaint is worth real money over a career.

Who represents whom, and why it matters

The listing agent works for the seller. You are not their colleague in that transaction and you are not there to help them sell the house. You are there because you represent, or are working with, the buyer.

That distinction sounds obvious and gets blurred in practice, particularly by new agents who find the listing agent friendly and start volunteering things. Be careful what you share about your buyer's position, their motivation, their timeline and their maximum. Ordinary professional courtesy does not require disclosing anything that weakens your client's negotiating position.

Florida's framework for what an agent owes a party, including the default relationship, is covered in what a transaction broker is in Florida real estate. Understand which relationship you are in before you are standing in a kitchen making conversation.

Settle your own paperwork before you go

This is the part that has changed most in recent years and is the one thing not to improvise.

Before showing property to a buyer you need your arrangement with that buyer agreed in writing, covering what you are doing for them and how you are compensated. You also need to know how compensation will work in the specific transaction, because it is a matter to be agreed rather than assumed from the listing.

Practice on this has moved, and it is exactly the kind of thing where the answer is your broker rather than an article, because brokerage policy and current forms govern it. Ask your firm what its process is, use its forms, and have the conversation with the buyer early rather than at the third property. A brokerage with real transaction support makes this straightforward, which is part of the argument in what transaction and admin support is worth.

How the money then reaches you is a separate mechanism again, and it always runs through your own broker, per how and when agents actually get paid after a closing.

The properties that sit outside the system

Not everything for sale is in the MLS, and those cases need a conversation rather than a booking.

For sale by owner. An owner selling without an agent has made no cooperative arrangement with anybody. You can approach them on behalf of your buyer, but whether they will work with an agent, and on what terms, has to be established directly and in advance. Never show a client through a property on the assumption it will be sorted out later.

New construction. Builders frequently have their own process, and many require the agent to accompany the buyer on the first visit for the agent to be recognized in the transaction at all. This is the single most expensive mistake a new agent makes on this subject: a buyer wanders into a model home alone on a Sunday, and the agent's role in the deal evaporates. Tell buyers early, before they go sightseeing.

Auction, foreclosure and estate sales. Each has its own procedure, and the assumption that ordinary showing arrangements apply is usually wrong.

The honest bottom line

Showing other brokerages' listings is not a special permission, it is the job. The MLS exists so firms cooperate, and a buyer's agent works with other companies' inventory almost all of the time.

What deserves care is your own side of it: written agreement with your buyer before you start, clarity about compensation in advance, discretion about your client's position in front of the listing agent, and a direct conversation before showing anything that sits outside the MLS.

Two situations that follow a showing: a buyer who wants to offer well under asking, and an unrepresented buyer who wants to buy your own listing.

Two things that follow a lot of showings: a buyer who never offers, and an agent on the other side who will not respond.

Before any of this comes up, there is a document that now has to exist: since August 2024 an MLS participant needs a signed written buyer brokerage agreement before touring a home with a buyer, whoever holds the listing.
