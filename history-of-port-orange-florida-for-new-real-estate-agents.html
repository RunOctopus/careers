The History of Port Orange, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Port Orange

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Port Orange's history runs through three distinct eras that still shape the town today. First came Dunlawton, a sugar and indigo plantation granted to Patrick Dean in 1804 and burned by Seminole forces in 1836, the ruins of which still stand on Old Sugar Mill Road. Then, in 1866, Dr. John Milton Hawks brought roughly 500 formerly enslaved people to the same stretch of river to found a freedom colony, first called Orange Port and then Port Orange, where his wife Dr. Esther Hill Hawks ran one of Florida's first integrated schools before the colony collapsed and the schoolhouse itself was burned. Some settlers stayed, building the community known as Freemanville. Finally, the Daytona-adjacent suburb agents sell today, built around the Dunlawton Avenue corridor, took shape only in the 1970s through 2010s. All three eras are real, documented, and still visible in the town's streets, names, and landmarks.

Key takeaways
  • Port Orange's oldest history predates the town's name by six decades: the Dunlawton sugar plantation, granted to Patrick Dean in 1804, was burned by Seminole forces on January 17, 1836, and its ruins still stand at Dunlawton Sugar Mill Gardens.
  • Dr. John Milton Hawks brought roughly 500 formerly enslaved people to the Halifax River in 1866 to found a freedom colony, first named Orange Port and then renamed Port Orange in 1867, but poor soil and corruption reduced it to nine families by 1869.
  • Esther Hill Hawks ran what is believed to be one of Florida's first integrated schools out of the colony, teaching 25 students, Black, white, and mixed race, together by November 1867, before the schoolhouse itself was burned down in January 1869.
  • The freedpeople who stayed built the community known as Freemanville near today's Orange and Charles Streets, and Mount Moriah Baptist Church, built in 1911, is the last structure standing from that settlement.
  • Port Orange incorporated as a town in 1913 and a city in 1926, but stayed genuinely small for half a century, not reaching 2,000 residents until after 1960.
  • The Daytona-adjacent suburb agents sell today, anchored by the Dunlawton Avenue corridor and The Pavilion at Port Orange, took shape only in the 1970s through 2010s, decades after the town's founding.

Before any of it: the Timucua and the Halifax River

Long before any plantation or freedom colony rose on the west bank of the Halifax River, this land was Timucua territory, part of the same river system that supported Mocama-speaking Timucua communities up and down Florida's east coast for centuries. Spanish explorers documented Timucua presence in the region as early as the early 1600s, and the Halifax River itself, the tidal lagoon Port Orange sits along today, was a working part of that world long before it was a boating and fishing amenity on a listing sheet. The Timucua did not survive as a distinct people past the colonial era, reduced by disease and conflict following European contact. An agent selling a home on the Halifax today doesn't need to become an archaeologist, but the water view didn't start as a view. It started as a river people relied on to live.

Indigo, sugar, and the name Dunlawton (1804 to 1832)

The first documented commercial development on the land that is now Port Orange came in August 1804, when the Spanish Crown granted merchant Patrick Dean, working with his uncle John Bunch, a planter based in Nassau, 995 acres on the west bank of the Halifax River. Dean built an indigo and sugarcane operation there, one of a string of plantations Spanish and, later, American planters carved out of this coastline in the early 1800s. Dean did not live to see it flourish. He was killed during the First Seminole War in 1818, and the property passed through a series of owners before land brokers sold it in 1832 to Sarah Anderson and her two sons, George and James. It was Sarah Anderson who gave the plantation the name that still labels a major Port Orange thoroughfare, a middle school, and a historic district today: she combined her maiden name, Dunn, with the surname of the land dealers who brokered the sale, Lawton, into Dunlawton. Under the Andersons, the plantation ran on enslaved labor, processing sugar, molasses, and rum for the regional market. An agent working Port Orange today drives past that name constantly, Dunlawton Avenue is the town's main commercial spine, without necessarily knowing it comes from a 19th-century land deal on a plantation worked by enslaved people. It's worth knowing where the name actually came from.

Burned in the Second Seminole War (1836)

The Dunlawton plantation's run under the Andersons was short. When the Second Seminole War broke out in December 1835, Florida's east coast sugar plantations became targets, and on January 17, 1836, a large band of Seminole warriors attacked Dunlawton. A Florida militia company known as the Mosquito Roarers, under Major Benjamin Putnam, rode to defend the plantation but arrived to find the Andersons' main house and the enslaved workers' quarters already burning. The militia exchanged heavy fire with the Seminole force but could not retake the property. The raid didn't end there. Those same militia fighters retreated to the nearby Bulow Plantation, took its owner John Bulow into custody on his own land, and fortified his house as a defensive position, only to see Bulowville burned as well less than two weeks later, on January 31, 1836. Dunlawton, like most of the sugar plantations along this stretch of coast, never recovered as a working plantation after 1836. The ruins of the sugar mill, coquina and brick walls, iron kettles, and the original cane press, still stand today at what is now Dunlawton Sugar Mill Gardens, a county park on Old Sugar Mill Road listed on the National Register of Historic Places. For an agent, the takeaway isn't just a battle date. Port Orange's oldest visible landmark is a ruin, not a restoration, a genuine physical link to a war that ended the plantation economy here three decades before the town even had its name.

Freedom on the Halifax: Dr. Hawks and the Orange Port colony (1866 to 1869)

Three decades after Dunlawton burned, the same stretch of the Halifax River became the site of one of Reconstruction-era Florida's more remarkable, and more difficult, experiments. Dr. John Milton Hawks, a Union Army surgeon and committed abolitionist, organized the Florida Land and Lumber Company in 1866 and brought roughly 500 formerly enslaved men and women, many of whom had served the Union cause during the war, up from wartime settlements to homestead public land along the river and work Hawks's sawmill. Hawks moved the settlement to its permanent site in February 1867 and named it Orange Port, but within two months he learned another town in the country already used that name, so by April 1867 the settlement's name had flipped to Port Orange, the name that stuck. The ambition behind the colony was real: land and wage work for people who, a year earlier, had owned neither their labor nor themselves. So were the obstacles. The soil along the river was thin and sandy, poorly suited to the farming the settlers needed to sustain themselves, and supplies meant for the colony were, by multiple historical accounts, siphoned off by corruption before they reached the people who needed them. Within two years, the population Hawks had recruited had collapsed to just nine families still on the land by 1869. This is the harder half of Port Orange's founding story, and it belongs in the record alongside the town's name change: the same year Port Orange got the name it still carries, the colony that gave the area its first real population was already failing.

Esther Hill Hawks's school, and what happened to it (1867 to 1869)

Alongside her husband, Dr. Esther Hill Hawks, a physician in her own right at a time when very few women practiced medicine in America, took on the settlement's education. Teaching for the Freedmen's Aid Society, she is believed to have run one of Florida's first integrated schools, admitting Black and white children and adult freedpeople from the colony into a single classroom, an arrangement almost unheard of anywhere in the postwar South. The schoolhouse itself wasn't even finished when she started teaching. For months, Esther Hawks held class outdoors, building a log fire to keep her students warm through the winter of 1867. Her persistence produced real results: by November 19, 1867, she reported a flourishing school of 25 students learning together, eight white children, fifteen Black students, and two children of mixed race. It didn't last. The colony's broader collapse strained the school through 1868, and in January 1869 the schoolhouse itself was torched, destroyed by fire rather than lost to accident, according to the historical record. Esther Hawks kept teaching in the settlement a while longer before returning to New England in 1870 to resume practicing medicine there. For an agent, this is not a detail to soften or skip. It is the town's actual founding education story: a genuinely integrated classroom, in Florida, barely four years after the Civil War ended, that someone thought was worth burning to the ground.

What Freemanville left behind

Even after the Florida Land and Lumber Company's colony effectively collapsed, not everyone left. A number of the freedpeople who had come north with Hawks stayed on the land near what are now Orange Avenue and Charles Street in Port Orange, and the community they held together became known, unofficially but persistently, as Freemanville. In 1911, more than four decades after the original colony's collapse, that community built Mount Moriah Baptist Church, and it still stands today, widely recognized as the last remaining structure from the original Freemanville settlement. The church was renovated in 1956, sits on the Florida Black Heritage Trail, and hosts an annual Freeman Day Ceremony commemorating the settlers' history. For an agent, Mount Moriah isn't a museum piece. It's an active congregation on a street a buyer might drive down on the way to a showing, direct physical continuity between an 1860s colony and the neighborhood that exists today, and part of the reason some longtime Port Orange families carry this history personally rather than as an item read off a plaque.

A slow, small town for half a century (1913 to 1960s)

After Freemanville's collapse, Port Orange spent decades as a modest agricultural community, citrus, lumber, and eventually boatbuilding and oystering along the river, before it formally incorporated as a town on May 7, 1913. Growth over the following decades was real but genuinely small: the town's population went from 380 residents in the 1920 census to 678 by 1930, driven mostly by local citrus and boatbuilding work rather than any single boom. Port Orange reincorporated as a city in 1926, giving it fuller municipal authority, but the Great Depression slowed growth through the 1930s regardless of the upgraded charter. Recovery came after World War II, when returning veterans and northern migrants looking for affordable housing near the Daytona Beach area's growing tourism economy pushed the population to 1,201 by 1950 and 1,801 by 1960. None of those numbers look dramatic today, but they're the reason Port Orange's oldest residential pockets, the ones nearest the river and the old Ridgewood Avenue corridor, read as genuinely older and more established than the subdivisions further west, and an agent should be able to tell a buyer which is which.

Dunlawton Avenue, the Pavilion, and a Daytona-adjacent suburb (1970s to 2010)

Port Orange's real transformation into the town agents work today happened west of the river, and it happened comparatively recently. When Dunlawton Avenue was extended west toward Interstate 95 in the 1970s, the town gained real east-west access for the first time, and the corridor that had once led only to the old sugar mill ruins became Port Orange's commercial spine. Daytona Beach Junior College, chartered by the State Board of Education in 1957 and renamed Daytona Beach Community College in 1971 before becoming Daytona State College in 2008, grew its presence in the area over these same decades, adding steady, enrollment-driven housing demand that had nothing to do with tourism or citrus. The corridor's growth culminated on Dunlawton Avenue itself with The Pavilion at Port Orange, a 415,000-square-foot retail and entertainment center that opened in 2010 more than 92 percent leased, bringing roughly 2,000 jobs and the town's first large-scale modern shopping destination. Combined, the extended highway access, the college, and the retail buildout are why Port Orange reads today as a Daytona-adjacent suburb organized around a commercial corridor rather than a historic downtown, a genuinely different physical pattern than DeLand or Flagler Beach, and one an agent should be able to explain when a buyer asks why Port Orange doesn't have an obvious Main Street.

Port Orange today, by the numbers

Port Orange's 2026 population stands at roughly 68,467, up from 62,734 at the 2020 census, a growth rate of just over 9 percent in five years layered on top of a town that took until 1960 to reach 1,801 residents. Median age is 46.9 (45.3 for men, 49.4 for women), meaningfully older than a typical young-family suburb but younger than a retirement town like Flagler Beach. Median household income runs about $74,426, with a poverty rate near 11 percent. The population today is roughly 85 percent White and just under 4 percent Black, a demographic profile shaped in part by exactly the history above: Freemanville was the area's first substantial Black community, and its collapse by 1869 is part of why that population never grew into a larger share of the town. None of this is trivia. It's the population an agent is actually serving today, layered on top of a place whose two founding populations, the enslaved and free labor at Dunlawton and the freedpeople of Freemanville, together built the town and largely didn't get to keep it.

Riverwalk, the Causeway, and the sugar mill's second life

Port Orange's public spaces today sit directly on top of its history. Riverwalk Park, along Ridgewood Avenue on the Halifax River, opened in November 2017, timed to the 150th anniversary of the town's 1867 naming, and offers a boardwalk, a kayak and paddleboard launch, and Fysh Bar and Grill overlooking the water. Port Orange Causeway Park offers fishing and boat access on the Intracoastal side of town. And the old Dunlawton sugar mill ruins had an unlikely second act: from roughly 1947 to 1952, the site operated as Bongoland, a roadside attraction built around a resident baboon and a set of oversized concrete dinosaur sculptures, including a 42-foot Tyrannosaurus rex, created by local artist M.D. Lawrence. Bongoland folded within five years for lack of visitors, but four of its dinosaurs, among them a 25-foot triceratops, still stand today at Dunlawton Sugar Mill Gardens, the same National Register site where the Anderson family's mill burned in 1836. It's a genuinely strange, genuinely real detail, war ruins turned roadside kitsch turned quiet public garden, and it's the kind of specific, verifiable local color that tells a buyer an agent actually knows the town rather than just its listings.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer choosing a home on the Dunlawton Avenue retail corridor, or driving past Mount Moriah Baptist Church on the way to a showing near Orange and Charles Streets, is moving through decisions and hardships that go back to an 1804 Spanish land grant, a war that burned a sugar mill in 1836, and a freedom colony that both succeeded, genuinely, in building an integrated school four years after the Civil War, and failed to survive as originally built. An agent who can explain why Dunlawton Avenue carries that name, why Freemanville still matters to some Port Orange families, and why the town's commercial spine only took shape in the 1970s and 2010s rather than a century earlier is offering something a market summary never can: an honest, accurate answer to why Port Orange looks and feels the way it does. That's a real, durable edge, not trivia recited for its own sake.
