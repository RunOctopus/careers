How to Tell a Good Brokerage From a Good Recruiter

Home›For Experienced Agents›Brokerage or Recruiter

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

By getting information from somewhere other than the person recruiting you. A recruiting conversation is the firm at its most attentive and best prepared, and it is genuinely uninformative about ordinary Tuesdays, because the qualities that make someone excellent at recruiting are not the qualities that make a firm good to work at. The fix is not suspicion, it is sampling: talk to an agent who joined this year, ask to see the systems working rather than described, and get the terms in writing. If the same picture survives all three, it is probably real.

Key takeaways
  • The recruiting conversation is the firm at its most polished. It tells you about recruiting, not about working there.
  • Attentiveness during recruitment predicts nothing about availability afterwards, because the incentives are different.
  • Get one sample the firm did not choose: an agent who joined this year, ideally someone you found yourself.
  • Ask to see things working rather than described. A demonstration is verifiable and a description is not.
  • None of this means recruiters are dishonest. It means one conversation is a bad sample of anything.

A recruiting conversation is a good experience by design. Someone experienced gives you their full attention, answers everything, describes a firm that sounds like the answer to your problems, and follows up promptly.

The difficulty is that this tells you almost nothing about what working there is like, and the reason is structural rather than moral.

Why the recruiting conversation is a bad sample

Three things are true at once, none of them requiring anybody to be dishonest.

You are meeting the firm at its most prepared. The room is tidy, the person is on time, the materials exist. That is the same reason a first date is a poor guide to a marriage: everybody performs, and everybody knows it, and the performance is still real while being unrepresentative.

The person is chosen for this task. Firms put their most personable, most articulate people into recruiting because it works. That person may be wonderful and may also be the individual you will interact with least once you have signed.

The incentives differ before and after. During recruitment, the firm's interest is your yes, so responsiveness is total. Afterwards, its interest is that you produce, which is not the same as being available to you. Attentiveness now genuinely does not predict attentiveness later.

So the recruiting conversation is worth having and is not evidence. Treat it as the claim, and then go and test the claim.

Test one: talk to somebody who joined this year

Ask for an introduction to an agent who joined in the last twelve months. Not a top producer of ten years, and not the recruiter.

A recent joiner is the only person who has experienced both sides: what was described to them, and what turned out to be true. Ask what surprised them, what they wish they had asked, and how long it took to feel productive.

Better still, find one yourself. Every firm can produce a happy person on request, and a name you sourced independently is a sample the firm did not select. This is not sneaky; it is the same thing a firm does when it checks a candidate's references, and the corresponding discipline is in what to ask references before joining.

Reluctance to make the introduction at all is itself a finding, and it appears in red flags when evaluating a brokerage for good reason.

Test two: ask to see it rather than hear about it

Descriptions cannot be checked. Demonstrations can.

Ask to see the CRM with real data in it. Ask to see the training calendar for the next two months, as it exists rather than as it is intended. Ask to see the technology performing the specific task just described, on the spot. Ask what the last three sessions actually were and who attended.

The response matters as much as the content. A firm that has genuinely built these things shows them without hesitation, because showing is easier than explaining. A firm that has not will offer to send something later. The full checklist of what to test this way is in what a brokerage actually provides.

Test three: require the terms in writing

Every financial element should exist as a document before you decide: split, desk fee, transaction fees, technology charges, what is included, what is billed, and what happens to a pending deal if you leave.

This is not distrust. It is that two people remember a verbal conversation about money differently, and only one of them has the paperwork. Anything that exists solely in conversation should be treated as not offered when you run the comparison in how to compare two brokerage offers side by side.

The specific claims that need testing

Four things get said in nearly every recruiting conversation and each has a verifiable version.

We give our agents leads. Ask how many a typical new agent received last month, how they are routed, and how many people are on the rotation. A rotation shared by more agents than the flow supports is not a lead source. See what lead generation support to expect.

Our training is excellent. Ask for the calendar and the name of who teaches it. A schedule is a commitment; an open door depends on you knowing what to ask, which when new you do not. See what to look for in a mentorship program.

My door is always open. Ask whether that person sells, and how many agents they support. Both answers change what the promise means, which is the point of why a non-competing manager changes everything.

Agents here earn well. Ask about the median rather than the top. A firm's best producer tells you the ceiling and nothing about the typical outcome.

What a good recruiter actually does

Worth saying clearly, because the point is not that recruiting is suspect.

A good recruiter tells you when the firm is a poor fit for you, because a mis-hire costs everybody. They answer a direct question directly, including when the answer is unflattering. They make introductions without being pushed. They do not manufacture urgency. And they are comfortable with you talking to other firms, because they expect to compare well.

If you encounter that, it is genuine evidence, since a person prepared to talk you out of joining is not simply selling. The opposite pattern, where questions produce redirection and every concern is met with reassurance rather than information, is the one to notice.

The mirror-image mistake

There is a failure in the other direction worth naming: assuming a firm that recruits poorly must be good underneath, and that polish is proof of emptiness.

Sometimes a disorganized recruiting process is simply a disorganized firm. A well-run business tends to be well run at the front door too. Do not reward incompetence by reading it as authenticity.

The test is not how smooth the conversation was. It is whether the claims survive independent checking.

The honest bottom line

The recruiting conversation is the beginning of your research rather than the end of it. Take what you are told, then get one sample the firm did not choose, ask to see two things working rather than described, and require the terms in writing.

If the same picture survives all three, it is probably accurate. And a firm that makes all three easy has told you something no pitch could.
