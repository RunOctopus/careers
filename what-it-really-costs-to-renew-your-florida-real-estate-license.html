What It Really Costs to Renew Your Florida Real Estate License

Home›Keep Your License Working›What Renewal Costs

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

The Florida state renewal fee itself is modest, modest, commonly around $64 for a sales associate and somewhat more for a broker, and the exact amount you owe appears in your own account in the state licensing portal. That fee is the smallest line item. The real two-year cost of keeping a license active includes your required education, which is a much larger number for a first renewal than a later one, plus local Realtor board, state association and national association dues, MLS access, and errors and omissions coverage if your brokerage does not include it. Agents who budget only for the state fee are budgeting for a fraction of the actual number.

Key takeaways
  • The state renewal fee is small, commonly around $64 for a sales associate. Trust the figure in your own licensing portal account, not any published estimate.
  • Education is the variable that moves the total. A first renewal requires 45 hours of post-license education for sales associates, far more coursework than the 14 continuing education hours required in later cycles.
  • Board, state association and national association dues plus MLS access are separate from renewal entirely, and for an actively selling agent they typically exceed the renewal cost itself.
  • Errors and omissions coverage may be included by your brokerage or billed to you. It is worth knowing which before you compare brokerages on split alone.
  • The most expensive version of renewal is the one you miss. Reactivation adds required education, and a voided license means re-qualifying from the beginning.

Ask an agent what renewal costs and you will usually get the state fee, because that is the number on the renewal screen. It is also the least significant number in the whole calculation. Here is the full picture, separated into what renewal actually is, what people mistakenly fold into it, and what happens to the total if you get the timing wrong.

The state fee: the part everyone quotes

Florida's renewal fee is modest. It commonly runs around $64 for a sales associate, with a broker paying somewhat more. The reason to be careful with any published figure, including this one, is that fee schedules are set by rule and change, and the version that matters is the one showing in your own account when you log in to the state licensing portal at MyFloridaLicense.com. Look it up rather than budgeting from an article.

That is the entire state-side cost of an on-time renewal. No annual fee, no per-transaction charge, one payment every two years.

The education: the part that actually moves the total

This is where the real variation lives, and it depends entirely on which renewal you are facing.

A first renewal requires post-license education: 45 hours for a sales associate, 60 hours for a broker. That is a substantial course, and course pricing across approved providers varies widely by format, whether the package includes exam attempts or instructor support, and how the provider bundles it. It is normal for this to cost several times the state renewal fee.

Every renewal after your first requires 14 hours of continuing education, split into 3 hours of core law, 3 hours of ethics and business practices, and 8 hours of specialty credit. Fourteen hours is a much smaller commitment than 45, and it prices accordingly. Many agents find their later renewals cost meaningfully less than their first one for exactly this reason.

One caution worth more than the price difference: buy the education on the requirement, not on the price. A package that totals the right number of hours without carrying the prescribed core law and ethics components does not satisfy Florida's requirement, and discovering that after your deadline is a far more expensive outcome than paying more for the correct package. Our page on the 45-hour post-license course covers the first-renewal requirement specifically.

What renewal is not, but gets billed around the same time

Several real costs of practicing get mentally filed under renewal because they arrive on a similar rhythm. They are separate obligations, they are usually larger, and confusing them for renewal fees is how agents end up with a budget that is off by an order of magnitude.

Local Realtor board, state association and national association dues. These are membership in a voluntary organization, not a state licensing requirement. You can hold and renew an active Florida license without any of them. If you are actively listing and selling, you will almost certainly carry them anyway. Combined, they commonly run to several hundred dollars a year, and the exact amount depends on your board. Our page on whether you need the board and MLS works through when this is genuinely optional.

MLS access. Often billed separately from board dues, often tied to board membership, and effectively non-optional if you are taking listings.

Errors and omissions coverage. Some brokerages carry it and include it. Some pass the cost to the agent per year or per transaction. This is a real number and it belongs in any honest comparison between brokerages. We cover the range on our page about what E and O insurance costs for Florida agents.

Brokerage fees. Desk fees, technology fees, and franchise fees continue regardless of where you sit in your renewal cycle. They are not renewal costs, but they are the largest recurring number for many agents and they belong in the same budget conversation.

What it costs to be inactive

A voluntarily inactive license still renews on the normal two-year cycle, still carries the continuing education requirement, and still costs the state fee. What changes is that the board, MLS and E and O costs typically fall away, since you are not practicing. For an agent stepping back for a period, that is the entire financial logic of the status: you keep the license alive for a small fixed cost instead of paying the full stack for access you are not using.

The expensive version: missing the deadline

The cheapest renewal is an on-time one. Every version of missing it costs more, and the increments are steep.

If you miss continuing education for a later renewal, your license goes involuntarily inactive and reactivation requires additional education on a ladder tied to how long you wait. Florida law sets it at 14 hours of a prescribed continuing education course to reactivate a license involuntarily inactive for 12 months or less, and 28 hours of a prescribed education course for one involuntarily inactive for more than 12 but fewer than 24 months. A license involuntarily inactive for more than two years automatically expires and becomes null and void. Our page on reactivating an involuntarily inactive license walks the whole ladder.

If you miss post-license education before your first renewal, the license is null and void under Florida law rather than inactive, and the ordinary route back is re-qualifying from the beginning, exam included. That is not a fee, it is starting over.

And in every one of these cases there is a cost that never appears on an invoice: you cannot legally practice while your license is not active. For a producing agent, weeks of lost production dwarfs every fee on this page combined. That is the real cost of a missed renewal, and it is why the deadline matters more than the price of any course.

What is deductible

Most of the costs on this page are ordinary business expenses for a self-employed agent, which changes the effective number meaningfully. A fair way to compare brokerages on this

Renewal costs are roughly the same wherever you hang your license, since they are set by the state. What differs between brokerages is everything stacked around them: whether E and O is included or billed, whether board and MLS dues are absorbed anywhere, whether there are desk and technology fees, and whether anyone in the office is tracking your renewal date at all.

Those are answerable questions, and a brokerage that gives you clear numbers on all of them is telling you something useful about how it operates. One that describes its offer purely as a commission split is leaving the parts that cost you money out of the comparison.

Fee amounts, dues and course pricing vary and change over time. Confirm current figures with the DBPR, your local board, and your own tax professional. Educational only, not legal or tax advice.
