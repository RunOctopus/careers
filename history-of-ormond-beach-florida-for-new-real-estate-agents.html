The History of Ormond Beach, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Ormond Beach

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Ormond Beach began as Damietta, a cotton and indigo plantation the Ormond family carved out of a 2,000-acre Spanish land grant in the early 1800s, then was resettled in 1875 by a colony of transplants from New Britain, Connecticut, who incorporated the town as Ormond in 1880. What made it matter came next: the St. Johns and Halifax Railway reached town in 1886, John Anderson and Joseph Price opened the Ormond Hotel in 1888, and Henry Flagler bought it a year later and turned it into a playground for Gilded Age wealth. That wealthy winter-resort identity is the same reason John D. Rockefeller Sr. made The Casements his winter home starting in 1918, and the same reason the hard-packed sand out front became the birthplace of automobile land-speed racing between 1902 and 1935, a lineage that runs in an unbroken line to today's Daytona International Speedway. Ormond Beach didn't become an upscale, history-conscious coastal town by accident. It was built, deliberately, as one, and that origin still shapes who chooses it today.

Key takeaways
  • Ormond Beach's land was first a plantation called Damietta, developed by Captain James Ormond I on a 2,000-acre Spanish land grant, and the town still carries the Ormond family name today.
  • The modern town began as a colony of New Britain, Connecticut settlers in 1875 and formally incorporated as the Town of Ormond in 1880, a deliberate transplant community rather than an organic Florida settlement.
  • The Ormond Hotel, opened in 1888 and expanded by Henry Flagler starting in 1890, made Ormond Beach a Gilded Age winter destination for serious wealth, decades before the rest of the coast caught up.
  • John D. Rockefeller Sr. wintered at the Ormond Hotel starting in 1914, then bought The Casements in 1918 and lived there until his death in 1937, cementing the town's identity as a place where real money spent its winters.
  • The same hard-packed beach sand that drew Rockefeller's circle also drew automobile speed trials starting in 1902, earning Ormond Beach the title Birthplace of Speed and setting up the lineage that eventually produced NASCAR and the Daytona 500.

Before any of it: the Timucua and the village of Nocoroco

Long before any Ormond, Anderson, Price, or Rockefeller set foot here, the land where the Tomoka River meets the Halifax River was home to Nocoroco, a Timucua village that Spanish explorer Alvaro Mexia documented on an expedition in 1605. Archaeologists believe Nocoroco formed from two distinct Timucua clans that merged into one settlement, a theory supported by two different pottery styles found at the site. Centuries of habitation left a massive shell midden, built from generations of discarded oyster and clam shells, that still rises along the riverbank today. That site is preserved within Tomoka State Park, just north of downtown Ormond Beach, a short drive from neighborhoods like Tomoka Oaks that back up to the same river system. An agent doesn't need to become an archaeologist to sell property here, but it's worth knowing that the waterfront character buyers respond to today, the river, the oaks, the shell-lined banks, was exactly what drew people to this land a thousand years before it had a plat map.

Damietta: the plantation the town's name actually comes from

Ormond Beach's name isn't a marketing invention. It comes from Captain James Ormond I, a Scottish-born sea captain who had run a plantation in the Bahamas before securing a Spanish land grant of roughly 2,000 acres along the Halifax River in the early 1800s. He and his family developed the tract into Damietta Plantation, a working cotton and indigo operation, one of the more productive plantations in the region at the time. James Ormond I died in 1817 (some records say 1819), and his son, James Ormond II, kept the plantation running until his own death in 1829. The remains of that era are honestly small: a family tomb preserved today at James Ormond Tomb Park is essentially the physical anchor of the plantation era, since the Second Seminole War and decades of change erased most of what came after it. For an agent, the takeaway isn't the plantation itself, it's that the town's name is a direct, documented link to a specific family and a specific piece of land, not a generic coastal branding choice, and it's part of the honest, full record of how Florida's coast was settled, plantation economy included.

A Connecticut colony builds a town on purpose

The modern town has almost nothing to do with the Ormond family beyond the name. Around 1875, a colony of settlers from New Britain, Connecticut arrived and resettled the area, naming their new community New Britain after the town they'd left behind. Growth was gradual through the 1870s, but the settlers wanted a real, incorporated town, and on April 22, 1880, meeting in the upstairs hall of the Dix House (known locally as Dix Hall), the colony voted to incorporate as the Town of Ormond, choosing to honor the original Ormond family name rather than keep New Britain. That's a genuinely deliberate choice worth sitting with: transplants from New England built this town on purpose, the way Henry DeLand built DeLand or Isaac Moody platted what became Flagler Beach, and then chose to name it after the family who'd farmed the land two generations earlier rather than after themselves. It tells an agent something real about the town's self-image from day one: intentional, a little formal, aware of its own history even while making new history.

The railroad, the bridge, and the hotel that changed everything

Incorporation alone didn't make Ormond a destination. The St. Johns and Halifax Railway reached town in 1886, and the first bridge across the Halifax River followed in 1887, finally connecting the mainland settlement to the beach side. That same year, John Anderson and Joseph Price built the Ormond Hotel, which opened its doors on January 1, 1888, and quickly became one of the largest wooden structures in Florida. Two years later, in 1890, Henry Flagler, already building the Florida East Coast Railway empire that would define the state's coastline, bought the hotel and expanded it to accommodate roughly 600 guests, turning it into one of the best-known resort hotels in the country. The hotel eventually closed and was demolished in 1992, a real loss, but its builders are still honored today: the Anderson-Price Memorial Building, built in 1915 and now home to the Ormond Beach Historical Society, carries both their names. For an agent, this is the moment the town's character actually formed. Ormond Beach wasn't built as a fishing village that later found tourism; it was built, from the railroad up, as a wealthy winter destination, and that DNA is still visible in how the historic district and riverfront properties present themselves today.

Birthplace of Speed: the beach that started motorsports

The Ormond Hotel's wealthy winter guests needed something to do, and the hard-packed sand along the beach turned out to be close to ideal for the newest obsession of the era: the automobile. In 1902, retired businessman J.F. Hathaway began sending photographs of the beach to automobile journals and newspapers, promoting its hard, flat sand as an ideal race course. The first sanctioned meet followed March 26 to 28, 1903, with the American Automobile Association bringing timing equipment and a field that included Alexander Winton's Bullet and Ransom E. Olds' Pirate. On January 26, 1906, the steam-powered Stanley Rocket set a land-speed record of 127.659 mph on the sand, and in 1907, Glenn Curtiss set an unofficial world record of 136.36 mph on a V-8 motorcycle. Between 1903 and 1935, at least thirteen organized speed events were held on this stretch of beach, and John D. Rockefeller himself, wintering nearby, was known to come out and watch the drivers he socialized with at the hotel. The racing eventually outgrew the beach: by the 1930s promoters, Bill France among them, shifted competition to a combined beach-and-street course further south in Daytona, which led directly to the founding of NASCAR in 1947 and the first Daytona 500 in 1959. Ormond Beach doesn't just have a fun nickname; it is, by a documented, traceable line, where the sport actually started. That's why a brewery downtown, The Ormond Garage, builds its entire identity around honoring that racing history, and it's a genuine, differentiated selling point for buyers with any connection to motorsports, along with something worth knowing before a showing: Daytona International Speedway is close enough that agents should be ready to talk about Bike Week and Daytona 500 traffic and noise honestly.

Rockefeller and The Casements: the winter estate that sealed the town's identity

John D. Rockefeller Sr., the retired Standard Oil magnate and Flagler's former business partner, first started wintering at the Ormond Hotel in 1914. After four seasons there, he bought a private residence in 1918: The Casements, a home built in 1913 by Reverend Dr. Harwood Huntington and named for its casement-style windows. Rockefeller made it his winter home for the rest of his life, playing golf, watching the beach races, and hosting friends that reportedly included Will Rogers, Harvey Firestone, Thomas Edison, and Henry Ford. He died in his bedroom at The Casements in May 1937, just short of his 98th birthday. The estate passed out of the family within a couple of years and cycled through a school, a rest home, and a hotel annex before the City of Ormond Beach acquired it in the early 1970s; it was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1973 and today operates as the city's cultural center. For an agent, Rockefeller's decision to winter here, and to stay for two decades, is the single clearest proof point that Ormond Beach's wealthy-retreat identity wasn't hype. It's also a useful frame for today's market: the seasonal, affluent-retiree buyer choosing Ormond Beach for its winters is doing, in a smaller way, exactly what Rockefeller did a century ago.

Liberia and Sudan: the town's other founding communities

Ormond Beach's history includes communities that don't show up in the hotel-and-racing story, and they're part of the honest record. Starting in the 1880s and 1890s, formerly enslaved people and their descendants settled two neighborhoods within the town, naming them Liberia and Sudan after the African homelands their ancestors had been taken from. For generations, residents knew the area south of Washington Street as Liberia and the area north of it, toward Granada Boulevard, as Sudan. Those names weren't accidents or nicknames assigned by outsiders; they were chosen by the residents themselves as a direct, deliberate link to ancestry. In recent years the city has installed signage marking both communities, and the annual “I Am Ormond Beach” reunion celebrates that history and the African ties behind it. This matters for the same reason it matters in every Florida town with a documented Black founding community: some longtime Ormond Beach families carry this history directly, and an agent who knows the real place names and their origins, rather than treating them as trivia, is better equipped for honest, informed conversations with the community being served.

From Town of Ormond to City of Ormond Beach

The town incorporated in 1880 carried the plain name Ormond for seven decades. That changed on April 25, 1950, when a referendum formally renamed the municipality the City of Ormond Beach, a small but telling update that tied the town's identity more explicitly to the beach that had built its economy since the 1890s. The renaming came at the front edge of the real growth: the town's 1890 population was recorded at just 239 people. By the 2020 census it had grown to 43,080, and current estimates put it at roughly 45,000. That is not a straight line of slow, steady growth the way some inland Florida towns experienced it; it's the story of a small, deliberately built resort hamlet that took most of a century to become a genuine mid-size city, largely on the strength of the wealthy-retreat identity the hotel, the races, and Rockefeller had already established decades earlier.

Ormond Beach today, by the numbers

Ormond Beach's median age runs around 53 to 54, meaningfully older than a typical Florida coastal suburb, and consistent with a town whose defining historical identity was a winter retreat for people with the means and time to spend a season somewhere warm. Median household income sits around $69,000, with a poverty rate near 10 percent. The population is roughly 80 to 84 percent White, with Black, Asian, Hispanic, and multiracial residents each making up smaller shares, a demographic picture shaped in part by the same Liberia and Sudan communities that have called the town home since the 1880s and 1890s. None of this is trivia for an agent: an older, higher-income buyer base with deep seasonal-resident habits is a very different market to work than a young-family suburb, and it traces in a straight line back to the town Flagler and Rockefeller built.

Where Ormond Beach actually lives today

The history isn't locked in a museum; it shapes real neighborhoods agents show every week. Tomoka Oaks, built around an 18-hole golf course along the Tomoka River near the old Nocoroco site, offers roughly 500 homesites with river access for kayaking and fishing. Breakaway Trails, a gated community off Tymber Creek Road, trades river frontage for quick access to I-95 and the beach, with most sections sitting in a low-risk flood zone. Halifax Plantation, further inland near Tomoka State Park, anchors a cluster of golf and country club communities that reflect the same leisure-oriented identity the town has carried since its resort-hotel days. None of these are historic districts in the DeLand or Flagler Beach sense, but each one is a direct descendant of the same golf-course-and-river leisure culture that Ormond Beach has marketed to buyers, in one form or another, for well over a century.

Where Ormond Beach actually eats

Downtown Ormond Beach's dining scene sits along the same historic Granada Boulevard corridor that anchors the town's Victorian-era architecture. Rose Villa Southern Table & Bar occupies a building that operated as a bed and breakfast in the early 1900s, now serving Southern-inspired food including a well-known fried chicken. The Ormond Garage, a craft brewery and American-style eatery, builds its entire concept around honoring the town's racing history. 31 Supper Club regularly draws Central Florida “best of” recognition, Hull's Seafood Market & Restaurant is a longstanding local favorite for fresh catches, and Alexander's Prime offers dry-aged steaks and locally sourced seafood. For an agent, a historic downtown with this kind of real, independent dining is a genuine lifestyle selling point, particularly for buyers weighing Ormond Beach against louder, more commercialized stretches of the coast.

What there actually is to do in Ormond Beach

Tomoka State Park preserves both the Nocoroco archaeological site and genuine river access for kayaking, fishing, and hiking, a short drive from downtown. The Ormond Memorial Art Museum and Gardens anchors the town's cultural side with rotating exhibits and formal gardens. The Casements itself, Rockefeller's former estate, is open to the public as the city's cultural center and hosts tours and events year-round. Downtown's Granada Boulevard corridor supports walkable shopping and the dining already covered above. Layer in the town's ongoing relationship to its own racing history, memorialized at The Ormond Garage and in local markers, and Ormond Beach offers a real, varied answer to what daily life looks like here, not just a beach to look at once.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer touring a river-view home near Tomoka Oaks, or choosing a historic bungalow near Granada Boulevard over a newer build further inland, is responding to decisions made by the Ormond family, a colony of Connecticut transplants, Henry Flagler, and John D. Rockefeller, whether they know those names or not. An agent who can explain why the town reads as older, wealthier, and more history-conscious than its neighbors, and why a beach known for hard-packed sand ended up starting American motorsports, is offering something a generic market summary never can: a real answer to why this town feels the way it does. That's a genuine, durable edge with buyers who are choosing Ormond Beach on purpose, the same way its earliest residents did.
