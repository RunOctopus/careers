How Long Does It Take to Transfer Your Florida Real Estate License?

Home›For Experienced Agents›How Long a Transfer Takes

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

The state portion is quick. Once you submit the change of employing broker through the state licensing system and your new broker confirms it on their end, the update is commonly reflected within a business day or two. That is not what makes a brokerage move take weeks. The real timeline is set by everything around the license record: how fast your new brokerage completes its own onboarding, how your active listings and pending transactions get resolved with your current broker, and how quickly your board and MLS access is re-established, which in Volusia County can involve a different association entirely.

Key takeaways
  • The license record change itself is commonly reflected within a business day or two once both brokers have acted.
  • Your new broker has to confirm the change on their end, so their responsiveness is the gating factor on the state side.
  • Listings and pending deals are the slowest part of most moves, and they are negotiated rather than processed.
  • MLS and board access is a separate system with its own timeline, and it is what determines whether you can actually work on day one.
  • There is no state waiting period. Nothing about Florida licensing law requires you to sit out between brokerages.

Agents asking this question are usually asking two things at once: how long until my license record updates, and how long until I can actually work. Those have very different answers, and the gap between them is where most of the frustration in a brokerage move lives.

The state side: a day or two

The mechanics are genuinely quick. You submit a change of employing broker through the state licensing system, your new broker confirms it on their end, and the record updates. That update is commonly reflected within a business day or two once both sides have acted.

There is no state waiting period. Nothing in Florida licensing law requires an agent to sit idle between brokerages, and there is no cooling-off interval to serve. Our page on transferring your license to a new broker covers the transaction step by step.

The one dependency worth flagging is that this is a two-party transaction. Your new broker has to act. If they are slow to confirm, the state record sits waiting, and there is nothing you can do from your end to complete it alone. A brokerage that handles transfers routinely will do this the same day. That responsiveness during onboarding is a reasonable early signal of how the operation runs generally.

Why the real move takes longer

Here is what actually consumes the calendar.

Onboarding at the new brokerage. Paperwork, an independent contractor agreement, compliance setup, systems and CRM access, marketing and branding, headshots and materials. A brokerage with a defined onboarding process moves through this in days. One without it can take weeks and you will feel every one of them, because you are not fully operational until it is done. Our page on a fair onboarding timeline covers what good looks like here.

Listings and pending transactions. This is usually the genuine bottleneck, and it is not a processing delay, it is a negotiation. Active listings belong to the brokerage rather than to you, so moving one requires your current broker's cooperation and generally the seller's written consent. Pending deals are commonly closed out at the original brokerage or transferred by agreement. None of this runs on a clock you control. Our pages on commission on pending deals and what a broker can and cannot hold onto cover what is actually at stake.

Board and MLS access. This one surprises people, and locally it is the most likely thing to delay your first day of real work. Your MLS access is tied to your membership and your brokerage, not to your license, so it is a separate transition with its own timeline. In Volusia County it can be more than a timeline question, because the county is split across multiple associations that do not all run the same MLS. Moving to a brokerage anchored in a different part of the county can mean a different association entirely. Our page on MLS access when you switch brokerages here covers exactly what moves and what does not.

A realistic sequence

Rather than a single number, here is the order that keeps the total short.

Before you give notice: read your independent contractor agreement, decide with your incoming brokerage how listings and pending deals will be handled, and confirm what your board and MLS situation will be at the new office. This is the preparation that determines whether the move takes one week or six.

Notice and submission: tell your current broker directly and in writing, then submit the change of employing broker. Have your new broker ready to confirm immediately.

The first week after: onboarding, systems, and board and MLS re-establishment run in parallel. Verify your own license record in the state licensing portal at MyFloridaLicense.com rather than assuming it went through.

Ongoing: resolving pending transactions from the old brokerage often extends past everything else, and that is normal. Deals under contract at your former brokerage do not need to be finished before you start producing at the new one.

What does not add time

Two things worth ruling out, because both cause unnecessary delay when agents assume otherwise.

Your license renewal. Your renewal date and your employing broker are separate records. Transferring does not reset your expiration or your education requirement, and a renewal coming up is not a reason to postpone a move. Our page on switching before or after renewal covers the sequencing.

A fee or processing queue. Changing your employing broker is a record update rather than a new license application, and it is typically free. Our page on whether transferring costs anything covers the detail. You are not waiting in line behind an application review.

The gap to avoid

The one timing error with real consequences is leaving before your new brokerage is ready. A Florida sales associate license needs an active employing broker on file to be active, so a gap between brokers is a gap in your ability to legally practice.

This is why the sequence matters more than the speed. Line up the new brokerage completely, have them ready to confirm the change, and then give notice. An agent who resigns first and finds a home second has created the only genuinely slow version of this process.

What to ask a prospective brokerage

Since their responsiveness is the gating factor, ask directly. How quickly do you confirm a change of employing broker? What does your onboarding take, start to finish? Who handles the board and MLS transition, and does that change if I am coming from a different association? Have you taken on agents with contested listings before?

Specific answers to those questions are worth more than a general assurance that the process is easy. A brokerage that can describe its own timeline precisely is telling you it has run this many times.
