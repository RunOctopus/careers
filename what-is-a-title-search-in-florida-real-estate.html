What Is a Title Search, and Why Does Every Florida Closing Need One?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Title Search in Florida

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

A title search is a review of public property records, conducted before closing, to confirm the seller actually has the legal right to sell the property and that there's nothing recorded against it that would block a clean transfer, like an unpaid lien, an old judgment, an unresolved easement dispute, or a break in the chain of ownership. In Florida, title searches are typically performed by title companies or real estate attorneys, and any problem the search turns up is called a cloud on title. It's a routine step on paper, but it's also the single most common reason a Florida closing gets delayed at the last minute.

Key takeaways
  • A title search reviews public records, deeds, liens, judgments, mortgages, easements, and more, to confirm the seller has clear, legal ownership to convey to the buyer.
  • In Florida, the search is typically run by a title company or a real estate attorney, usually as part of preparing the title insurance commitment for the transaction.
  • A 'cloud on title' is any recorded issue that raises doubt about ownership, an old lien, an unpaid contractor's claim, a missing heir's signature, or a boundary dispute, and it has to be resolved before closing can happen.
  • Even a home owned for decades can have a title problem, since old liens, unresolved estates, and paperwork errors from prior sales can sit dormant in the public record for years before a new title search surfaces them.
  • New agents need to understand this process because clients ask about it constantly, and delays caused by title issues are one of the most common reasons a closing date slips.

What is a title search in Florida real estate?

A title search is a detailed review of the public records tied to a specific piece of property, going back through the chain of ownership to confirm that the current seller actually holds legal title and has the right to sell it. The person or company doing the search is looking for anything recorded against the property that could interfere with a clean transfer: unpaid mortgages that were never properly released, tax liens, judgments against a prior owner, mechanic's liens from unpaid contractors, easements that restrict how the land can be used, boundary or survey disputes, and errors or gaps in the recorded deed history itself. If the search comes back clean, the buyer can be confident they're getting exactly what they're paying for: full, unencumbered ownership.

Who performs a title search in Florida

In Florida, a title search is typically run by a title company or a real estate attorney, most often as part of preparing the title insurance commitment for the transaction. Florida is one of a smaller number of states where attorneys are also commonly involved directly in the closing process, so it isn't unusual to see a real estate attorney's office running the search and handling the closing itself, alongside or instead of a dedicated title company. Either way, the person conducting the search pulls records from the county clerk's office, or an indexed title plant built from those records, and builds what's called an abstract, a chronological summary of every recorded document affecting that property's ownership.

What the search is actually looking for

A title search isn't a single database lookup. It's a chain-of-title review that traces ownership backward, often decades, confirming that each transfer of the property was properly recorded and that nothing along the way created a competing claim. Specific items a search checks include: outstanding mortgages or liens that were never satisfied and released in the public record, judgments recorded against a past owner that could attach to the property, unpaid property taxes or code enforcement liens, mechanic's liens filed by a contractor who wasn't paid for work on the home, recorded easements or rights-of-way that limit how the property can be used, and, in some cases, unresolved probate or divorce issues where an heir or ex-spouse never formally released their interest in the property.

What a 'cloud on title' actually means

When a title search turns up a problem, it's referred to as a cloud on title: any recorded claim, lien, or defect that creates real doubt about whether the seller can convey clear ownership. A cloud on title doesn't automatically mean the seller doesn't own the home; it means something in the public record raises a legitimate question that has to be resolved before a title company will insure the transfer. Common examples include an old satisfied mortgage that was never officially recorded as paid off, a lien from a contractor dispute that predates the current owner, a deed with a name misspelling or a missing signature from years earlier, or an heir from a prior owner's estate who never formally signed off on their share of the property. Some clouds are cleared quickly, a document gets re-recorded, a lien gets paid and released, a quitclaim deed gets signed. Others take longer and can genuinely delay or, in rare cases, kill a closing if the seller can't resolve the underlying issue.

Why this matters even on a home owned for decades

New agents sometimes assume title issues only come up on unusual properties, foreclosures, estate sales, or homes that changed hands quickly and messily. In reality, a home that's been owned quietly by the same family for thirty or forty years can absolutely turn up a real problem, because the search isn't just looking at the current owner, it's tracing every transfer in the chain back through prior decades. An easement granted to a utility company in the 1980s that was never properly indexed, a lien from a home improvement loan that got paid off but never formally released, or a survey discrepancy from before modern GPS-based surveys existed, can all sit dormant in the public record for years and only surface when a new title search is run. That's exactly why the search happens on every closing, not just the ones that look complicated on the surface.

How a title search connects to title insurance

The title search is the foundation for the title insurance commitment, the document a title company issues promising to insure the buyer's (and the lender's) ownership interest once the deal closes, based on what the search found. If the search is clean, the commitment moves forward with standard exceptions. If it turns up a cloud on title, the title company will typically require that issue be resolved, a lien paid and satisfied, a missing signature obtained, a document re-recorded, before it will issue a clean policy. This is also why title insurance and the title search are closely linked but not identical concepts: the search is the investigative work, and the insurance is the financial protection that follows from it, covering the buyer and lender against title problems that existed before the closing but weren't discovered during the search.

What this means for a new agent's day-to-day work

A new agent doesn't perform the title search personally, but understanding what it does and why it matters is genuinely useful in day-to-day client work. Buyers ask what a title search is and why they're paying for it. Sellers sometimes get surprised or defensive when a title company comes back with a question about an old lien or a name discrepancy on a decades-old deed, and a prepared agent can explain calmly that this is a normal part of the process, not an accusation of wrongdoing. And when a closing date is at risk of slipping because a title issue is being worked out, an agent who understands what's actually happening, rather than just relaying vague updates from the title company, is far more useful to a nervous client on either side of the deal.
