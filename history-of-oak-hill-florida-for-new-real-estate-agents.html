The History of Oak Hill, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Oak Hill

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Oak Hill is one of the oldest documented sites in the county, a Timucua village shown on a European map in 1564, and one of the strangest in modern governance: the city dissolved its own charter during the Great Depression and stayed unincorporated for 32 years before residents brought it back in 1962. That on-again, off-again relationship with growth is the real reason Oak Hill still feels like undeveloped Old Florida today.

A village on the map three centuries before Florida was a state

Long before any European settlement, the site of modern Oak Hill was home to a Timucua village called Surruque el Viejo. The village appears on a map drawn by French cartographer Jacques Le Moyne in 1564, based on his time with the French expedition to Florida, making it one of the earliest documented habitation sites on this stretch of the Atlantic coast. For an agent, that matters less as trivia and more as context: Oak Hill's waterfront along Mosquito Lagoon has been recognized as valuable, livable ground for close to five hundred years, not because a developer decided so recently, but because the land itself has always supported people.

Live oak, U.S. Navy ships, and why the timber mattered

In the late 1700s, timber cutters began working the area's dense forests of live oak, prized above nearly every other American hardwood for shipbuilding because of its weight, density, and resistance to rot. A settler named McHardy received a Spanish land grant here beginning in 1800, one of the area's first documented European claims. In the early 1800s, the Swift brothers set up an operation supplying the strong Southern live oak the U.S. Navy needed for its wooden warships. That early export economy, cutting a genuinely scarce natural resource and shipping it out by water, set a pattern Oak Hill has repeated in different forms ever since: citrus, fish, and now waterfront real estate, all resources pulled from the land and the lagoon rather than built from scratch inland.

Seminole Rest: shell mounds most of Florida destroyed, but Oak Hill kept

On the western shore of Mosquito Lagoon, just east of town, sits Seminole Rest, a National Register site (listed March 19, 1997) built around a group of ancient shell middens. Timucuan people used the primary mound between roughly 770 and 1100 AD, piling up the shells left over from a seafood diet until they formed the rise the site is named for. What makes Seminole Rest genuinely rare is what didn't happen to it: across pioneer-era Florida, most shell mounds like this one were dug up and crushed for road-building material. Oak Hill's survived. Today the site, now part of Canaveral National Seashore, also preserves a late-1800s home built by the Hatton Tumor family directly atop part of the midden, along with a caretaker's cottage, walkable on a short interpretive trail. For an agent, it's a concrete, visitable landmark, not just a documented fact, that backs up the same story the 1564 map tells: people have valued this exact shoreline for well over a thousand years.

Citrus, salt, and a working Civil War-era economy

Jacob Davy Mitchell founded a citrus business in the area in the early-to-mid 1800s; by 1887, roughly 220 acres of citrus groves were under cultivation nearby. During the Civil War years, a salt works operated in the area as well, meeting a real wartime need when imported salt became scarce across the blockaded South. When Henry Flagler's Florida East Coast Railway reached the area, it gave Oak Hill's citrus and fish a real route to market for the first time, turning what had been subsistence and local trade into a shippable commercial product.

The city that dissolved itself, then came back 32 years later

Oak Hill's strangest and most telling piece of history is civic, not economic. The City of Oak Hill was first chartered in 1927, right at the tail end of Florida's 1920s land boom. When the boom collapsed and the Depression set in, the city took the unusual step of petitioning the state to inactivate its own charter in 1930, effectively dissolving itself as an incorporated city rather than struggle on as one. Oak Hill spent the next three decades unincorporated. It wasn't until 1962 that residents petitioned to reactivate the charter and Oak Hill became a city again, this time by deliberate choice rather than boom-era momentum. Few towns in Florida can point to a documented, voluntary 32-year gap in their own incorporation. For an agent, that fact is a genuinely useful shorthand for the town's character: Oak Hill has never chased growth for its own sake, and its history shows a community willing to shrink back to what it could actually sustain rather than overextend.

What that history means for real estate today

Oak Hill's combination of deep, real history and a documented refusal to overbuild is exactly why it still reads as authentic, undeveloped Florida to buyers today, rather than a manufactured version of it. Waterfront along Mosquito Lagoon, known for its redfish flats fishing, commands real premiums for docks, boat lifts, and launch proximity, the same way access to the water has mattered here since Timucua families first settled the shoreline. Goodrich Seafood and Oyster House, an out-of-the-way fish house on the water operating since 1971, is the kind of living landmark that makes the case in person: this isn't a manufactured waterfront theme, it's a working one. An agent who can speak to that continuity, not just the current fishing culture but the centuries of real history behind it, has a genuine advantage with the buyer who specifically wants Old Florida and knows the difference between real and staged.
