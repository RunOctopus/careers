The History of New Smyrna Beach, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of New Smyrna Beach

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

New Smyrna Beach traces its name to 1768, when Dr. Andrew Turnbull, a Scottish physician, landed roughly 1,255 indentured Minorcan, Greek, and Italian colonists here to work what was then the largest single colonization venture in British North America. It was a real, well-documented, and genuinely brutal failure: by 1777, 964 of those colonists had died, and the survivors walked to St. Augustine and never came back. The plantation sat largely empty for decades, was burned again during the Second Seminole War in 1835, and was not incorporated as its own town until 1887, with a population of 150. The beachside community of Coronado Beach did not merge into it, and the town did not take the name New Smyrna Beach, until 1947. None of that is folklore, all of it is documented, and understanding it honestly, not as a marketing legend, is what separates an agent who can answer a buyer's real questions about the ruins downtown from one who is just repeating the nickname.

Key takeaways
  • New Smyrna Beach's name dates to 1768, when Dr. Andrew Turnbull founded a plantation colony here and named it for his wife's birthplace of Smyrna, in what is now Turkey.
  • The colony brought roughly 1,255 indentured Minorcan, Greek, and Italian settlers on a grant that grew to about 60,000 acres, the largest single British colonization attempt in North America at the time.
  • The colony's nine years were genuinely brutal: 964 colonists died by 1777 from disease, malnutrition, and overwork, and survivors documented mistreatment by Turnbull's overseers in sworn affidavits before fleeing to St. Augustine on foot.
  • The settlement then sat largely empty for decades, was burned again during the Second Seminole War on Christmas Day 1835, and was not incorporated as its own town until 1887, with a population of just 150.
  • The beachside community of Coronado Beach only merged into the mainland town, forming the New Smyrna Beach known today, in 1947, which is why the mainland and the barrier island still read as two different markets.
  • A decades-deep, genuinely earned surf culture, not a marketing invention, is why Flagler Avenue is lined with independent shops instead of chain resort development.

Before Turnbull: the Timucua and Turtle Mound

Long before any European set foot on this stretch of the Atlantic coast, it was Timucua territory. The local chiefdom that Spanish colonial records documented here was called Surruque, and its people lived along this coastline for roughly 2,000 years, leaving behind one of the largest shell middens in the mainland United States: Turtle Mound, a mound of oyster shell more than 600 feet long and over 50 feet high, built up over centuries of daily life. It sits about nine miles south of downtown New Smyrna Beach today, inside Canaveral National Seashore, and was added to the National Register of Historic Places on September 29, 1970. When Spain ceded Florida to Britain in 1763, most of the Timucua who remained left with the departing Spanish, effectively ending thousands of years of continuous Indigenous presence on this coast in a matter of months, five years before Turnbull's ships ever arrived. For an agent, Turtle Mound is worth knowing for a practical reason, not just a historical one: it survives today specifically because it sits inside a federally protected National Seashore, and that same protected status is a real part of why the undeveloped shoreline south of the inlet holds the kind of scarcity value it does. A buyer asking why the beach empties out south of town deserves an answer rooted in that protection, not a shrug.

A colony built at a scale nothing else in the colonies matched

The town's actual founding story begins in 1768 with Dr. Andrew Turnbull, a Scottish physician who had married Maria Gracia Rubini, a merchant's daughter born in Smyrna, in what is now Izmir, Turkey. Turnbull named his colony after her birthplace. He secured a 20,000-acre land grant from the British crown, then expanded it to roughly 60,000 acres by partnering with two other British investors, Sir William Duncan and George Grenville, to grow indigo at industrial scale for the London dye market, where it sold for as much as a dollar a pound. To work that land, Turnbull recruited about 1,100 indentured servants from Minorca, where a run of crop failures had left farmers desperate enough to sign contracts, along with roughly 200 more from Greece and about 100 from Italy, Corsica, France, and Turkey. On April 17, 1768, he assembled 1,403 colonists at Gibraltar and loaded them onto eight ships. It took nearly four months to reach Florida, and scurvy and shipboard infection killed 148 of them before the fleet ever landed; 1,255 arrived at Mosquito Inlet in August 1768. Unlike DeLand's Henry DeLand or Flagler Beach's Isaac Moody, single founders building a personal vision with their own money, New Smyrna's founding was a corporate venture, backed by multiple absentee British investors chasing an industrial-scale profit. That distinction matters for an agent explaining the town's origin: this wasn't one person's dream town, it was a commercial gamble run on other people's labor.

Nine hard years, told honestly

What happened next is genuinely difficult, well documented, and should not be softened into a quaint founding legend. Housing for the new arrivals existed for only about 500 people, far short of the 1,255 who needed shelter. The work was seven days a week with no rest days, in a climate and disease environment the settlers had never faced. Colonists died of starvation, scurvy, dropsy, yellow fever, typhoid, and pneumonia. Contracts promised indentured workers half the crops they raised and freedom after a set term; colonists later testified under oath that Turnbull's overseers routinely flogged workers for insufficient output and that Turnbull refused to honor contracts once they expired. Conditions improved somewhat between 1771 and 1773 with better harvests, then worsened again after severe droughts in 1773 and 1775 brought renewed hunger and a rising death toll. By 1777, nine years after the colony began, 964 of the roughly 1,255 who had arrived were dead, a survival rate of about one in three. This is not a “living legacy” story to soften for a marketing page. It is a real record of exploitation and mass death, and an agent who understands that is better equipped for the honest version of local history than one who only knows the nickname.

The 1777 collapse and the walk to St. Augustine

In March 1777, while Turnbull was away in England, a group of Minorcan colonists escaped the plantation and made the roughly 70 to 80 mile journey north to St. Augustine, the capital of British East Florida. By April, nearly 600 settlers had followed them and relocated to St. Augustine, effectively dissolving the New Smyrna settlement. On May 7, 1777, seventeen colonists drafted sworn affidavits for the British East Florida government, formally documenting the mistreatment they had endured. Governor Patrick Tonyn, rather than enforcing the colonists' original contracts and sending them back to Turnbull's plantation, allowed them to stay in St. Augustine and effectively released them from their indentures. Most of the Minorcan, Greek, and Italian settlers who survived never returned to New Smyrna. They became the founding population of St. Augustine's Minorcan community, which still identifies with that heritage today. For an agent, this explains something concrete: there is no continuous local family lineage tracing back to 1768 in New Smyrna Beach itself, the way some towns can point to a founding family's descendants still living there. The people who survived this town's founding left it, and rebuilt their lives somewhere else.

Decades of near-emptiness, then a second burning

After 1777, the former colony site sat largely abandoned. Ownership of the land passed through a series of absentee holders over the following decades, and only limited plantation activity, mostly small-scale indigo and later sugar operations, returned to the area in the early 1800s. Then, on Christmas Day 1835, at the outset of the Second Seminole War, Seminole warriors raided the area and burned the remaining plantation structures to the ground. The United States Army established a military post at New Smyrna in 1837 to reestablish some order, but few settlers came back to resettle permanently in the years that followed. This is the second time in less than seventy years that whatever settlement existed here was essentially wiped out and abandoned. For an agent explaining this town's history to a buyer, the honest framing is not one continuous 258-year-old town, it is a specific site that failed and emptied out twice before anything permanent took root.

Real resettlement, and a town incorporated at last

Meaningful, lasting resettlement did not begin until after the Civil War. Families slowly moved back into the area through the 1870s and 1880s, and on March 21, 1887, the Town of New Smyrna was formally incorporated with a population of 150. That 1887 incorporation, not the 1768 founding, is the real starting point for the town most agents actually sell today. The platted streets, the downtown commercial buildings, and the residential neighborhoods that define New Smyrna Beach's historic character almost all postdate this era. An agent describing a “historic” property downtown should be precise about which history it belongs to: the colonial period left ruins and archaeological sites, not standing houses, and the built environment buyers actually walk through dates from the 1880s onward.

1947: Coronado Beach merges in, and the town becomes two places at once

For decades, the mainland river town of New Smyrna and the barrier-island beach community of Coronado Beach grew up separately, on opposite sides of the Indian River, as two distinct places with two distinct identities. That changed in 1947, when the mainland town annexed Coronado Beach, merged the two communities, and adopted the name New Smyrna Beach, incorporating as a city in the process. For an agent, this is not a piece of trivia, it is the real origin of a split that still shapes the market today: mainland riverside neighborhoods and barrier-island beachside property were literally two separate incorporated identities until less than eighty years ago, and they still carry different architecture, different flood risk, and different price tiers as a result. Understanding that a buyer choosing “New Smyrna Beach” is actually choosing between two historically distinct halves of one city is a real, useful piece of local knowledge.

From a quiet fishing town to one of the world's best surf towns

New Smyrna Beach's surfing identity has real roots going back to the early 1960s, and it grew into something genuinely earned rather than manufactured for tourism. In the 1970s, local surfers Kem McNair and Charley Baldwin won East Coast surfing titles and put the town on the map within the sport. The 1988 Aloe Up Cup brought national attention, overhead surf, and major sponsorship to the New Smyrna Beach inlet, and the Orlando Surf Expo's growth through the 1990s kept the area connected to the sport's national scene. That history is documented in a permanent East Coast Surf Exhibit at the New Smyrna Museum of History downtown. In 2012, National Geographic named New Smyrna Beach one of the world's top 20 surf towns, and Surfer magazine has recognized it the same way. Professional surfers, including Kelly Slater, have surfed the breaks here during key stretches of their careers. For an agent, this is not marketing color. Decades of genuine surf culture are a direct, practical reason the businesses along Flagler Avenue skew toward independent surf shops, boutiques, and galleries rather than chain resort development, the same way Flagler Beach's deliberate height limits shaped that town's downtown, just from a different, more organic origin.

Flagler Avenue's five blocks

Flagler Avenue, named for Henry Flagler, the railroad magnate whose Florida East Coast Railway shaped so much of this coastline, runs just five blocks from the Intracoastal Waterway to the Atlantic Ocean. In that short stretch it packs in independent boutiques, surf shops, galleries, and restaurants, along with a packed annual calendar of art, music, food, and wine festivals. This walkability is not incidental. It is the physical result of the surf culture and the deliberate small-business character described above, and it is a genuine lifestyle premium for buyers who could choose a more generic beach town and are choosing this one instead. An agent who can speak specifically to which residential streets sit within true walking distance of Flagler Avenue, as opposed to a car ride away, is offering something a general market summary cannot.

New Smyrna Beach today, by the numbers

New Smyrna Beach's 2026 population stands at roughly 33,829, up from 30,456 at the 2020 census, an increase of about 11 percent over five years. The median age is 57.8 years (58.2 for men, 57.5 for women), reflecting a population that skews meaningfully older, similar in direction to Flagler Beach up the coast but attached to a larger, faster-growing city. Median household income is around $83,865, with a poverty rate of about 8.18 percent, consistent with a stable, largely owner-occupied coastal market rather than a low-income or transient one. None of this is trivia. It shapes which price points, property types, and buyer priorities actually move here, as distinct from younger, family-oriented markets inland.

Where New Smyrna Beach actually eats

The town's restaurant scene reflects its layered history as directly as its architecture does. Norwood's, one of the area's oldest continuously operating establishments, started in 1929 disguised as a Pan Am gas station and general store before becoming the restaurant it is known as today in 1946; a Treehouse Bar was added in 2015. JB's Fish Camp began as a shack on the river in the late 1970s, opened by John “JB” Bollman, and has grown into one of the town's most recognizable seafood spots, with river views and regular live music. For an agent, a food scene built on real, decades-old, family-run establishments rather than imported chains is a genuine lifestyle selling point for buyers weighing New Smyrna against a more generic coastal alternative.

What there is to do beyond the beach

Turtle Mound and the surrounding Canaveral National Seashore, already covered above, anchor the area's deep history and its protected, undeveloped shoreline. Smyrna Dunes Park, at the north end of the barrier island, offers boardwalk trails through a genuinely rare dune ecosystem where the Indian River and the Atlantic meet. The Marine Discovery Center runs eco-tours and kayak trips through the Indian River Lagoon, one of the most biodiverse estuaries in North America. Downtown, Old Fort Park holds the so-called Turnbull Ruins, coquina foundation walls five and a half feet thick whose original purpose, church, storehouse, or something else, remains genuinely debated by historians; the site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on July 10, 2008, as part of a multiple-property listing documenting the Turnbull colony's archaeological remains. The New Smyrna Museum of History, run by the Southeast Volusia Historical Society, houses both the colonial-era record and the surf exhibit described above. For an agent, being able to answer a buyer's question about what those ruins downtown actually are, honestly, including the fact that historians still disagree, is a small but real credibility marker.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer standing at the Turnbull ruins in Old Fort Park, or choosing a cottage a five-minute walk from Flagler Avenue, or weighing a beachside condo against a mainland riverfront lot, is responding to a genuinely layered history: a failed 18th-century colony that killed most of the people brought here to build it, a century of near-emptiness and a second burning, a real town that only incorporated in 1887, a merger that only made it a beach town on paper in 1947, and a surf culture that only became a defining identity in the decades since. An agent who can explain that layered, sometimes hard, history, rather than repeating a tidy nickname, is offering something a generic market summary never can: a real, honest answer to why this specific stretch of coast feels the way it does.
