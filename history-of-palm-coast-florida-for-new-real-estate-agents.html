The History of Palm Coast, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of Palm Coast

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Palm Coast does not have an old-town history in the way DeLand or Flagler Beach do, and that gap is itself the story. Starting in late 1968, the conglomerate ITT quietly assembled roughly 68,000 acres of Flagler County pine land and, on June 16, 1969, announced plans to build an entirely new planned community from scratch: a platted grid of streets, 48,000 home sites, and a 46-mile freshwater and 23-mile saltwater canal system, laid out years before most of the homes existed. Palm Coast did not become a city at all until residents voted to incorporate on September 21, 1999, with the change taking effect December 31, 1999. That planned, corporate, and very young origin, not a founder's biography or a lost fortune, explains the uniform lot grid, the near-total absence of a historic district, and why the real buyer conversation here is about HOA rules, canal classification, and new construction rather than architectural pedigree.

Key takeaways
  • Palm Coast was not settled organically. Starting in late 1968, ITT formed ten subsidiary companies to quietly buy roughly 68,000 acres from 35 landowners, then announced the planned community on June 16, 1969.
  • ITT platted more than 42,000 of those acres into 48,000 home sites, along with 46 miles of freshwater canals and 23 miles of saltwater canals, years before most of the homes were actually built.
  • The first residents did not move in until January 1972, but by 1975 more than 38,000 lots had already been sold, which is why platted, unbuilt, and resale-of-new-construction lots are still a normal part of the market here today.
  • ITT ran Palm Coast's roads, utilities, and services as a private, corporate government for 26 years before formally withdrawing in 1995, leaving a real governance vacuum.
  • Palm Coast did not exist as a city at all until voters chose to incorporate on September 21, 1999, with the change taking effect December 31, 1999, making it one of the newest municipal governments in the region.
  • Because nearly all of Palm Coast's built stock dates from 1972 forward, there is no historic district comparable to DeLand's Garden District or Flagler Beach's pier neighborhood, and an agent needs a different toolkit here: HOA documents and lot type, not architectural history.

Before Palm Coast: pine swamp, cattle, and a hunting lodge called Cherokee Grove

Long before any of this was Palm Coast, the land belonged to the Timucua, Mocama-speaking peoples of the same St. Johns River Valley corridor documented by Spanish explorers across this part of Florida as early as the early 1600s. After European contact, the Timucua did not survive as a distinct people, and no substantial permanent settlement replaced them here for a very long time. Into the 20th century, Flagler County stayed rural: cattle, citrus, timber, and turpentine, with the entire county's population still well under 5,000 as late as the 1960s. One parcel on what is now Palm Coast's western edge had a real, documented story of its own well before any conglomerate arrived. Henry Mason Cutting bought the tract in 1886, named it Cherokee Grove, and in 1887 built an Adirondack Camp-style hunting lodge there using local coquina, tabby, and cedar, reportedly including one of the first in-ground concrete swimming pools in Florida. After Cutting's death, his widow remarried a Russian prince-in-exile and took the title of princess herself, and the property became known as Princess Place, the name it still carries as a Flagler County preserve on the National Register of Historic Places. None of that history is part of Palm Coast's own municipal story. But it matters to an agent because it is the only real pocket of 19th-century built history anywhere near the city, and because of what happened there in June 1969.

A conglomerate buys 68,000 acres to build a city that did not exist yet

Starting in late 1968, the conglomerate ITT (International Telephone and Telegraph) began quietly assembling land in Flagler County, forming ten subsidiary companies to buy parcels from 35 separate owners without tipping its hand. The sellers included ITT's own Rayonier timber subsidiary, the Wadsworth Land Company, and the former Lehigh Portland Cement Company property, a plant that had employed roughly 300 people on what is now Colbert Lane from 1952 to 1965. By the time the buying was done, ITT held roughly 68,000 acres of mostly pine-covered swamp. On June 16, 1969, ITT held its formal public announcement, fittingly, at the historic lodge at Princess Place: plans for a wholly new planned community that would eventually be platted across more than 42,000 of those acres into 48,000 home sites. ITT put the master plan in the hands of William Levitt, the developer behind the Levittown suburbs, whose firm Levitt and Sons ITT had acquired in 1968, and Levitt's project manager, Dr. J. Norman Young, predicted the community would eventually house 750,000 residents, roughly 150 times Flagler County's entire population at the time. That number never came close to happening, and being straightforward about the gap between the sales pitch and reality is part of understanding this town honestly. But the ambition behind it, building an entire city from a corporate business plan instead of letting one grow up around a river landing or a rail depot, is the real, durable difference between Palm Coast and every other town in this history series.

Platted before it was built: the canal grid and the letter sections

What ITT actually built first was not houses. It was infrastructure: a network of 46 miles of freshwater canals and 23 miles of saltwater canals connecting to the Intracoastal Waterway, dredged and engineered so the excavated fill could elevate the surrounding home sites out of the swamp. The streets running through each platted section were deliberately named to share a common first letter, B-Section, C-Section, P-Section, and so on, a planning convention that made the development easy to sell and easy to manage at scale, and one that residents and agents still use today to describe where a property sits. That grid was built and sold well ahead of the homes that would eventually fill it. The first residents did not move into Palm Coast until January 1972, but by 1975, ITT's sales operation had already sold more than 38,000 lots. That gap between platted and sold versus actually built is the single most practical fact in this whole history for a working agent: it is why vacant, pre-platted lots and new construction on decades-old lots are still a completely normal, ongoing category of the Palm Coast market in a way they simply are not in an old town like DeLand or Flagler Beach.

Grand opening to first residents, 1970 to 1972

Palm Coast's public debut came at a grand opening on October 29, 1970, with then-Governor Claude Kirk giving the keynote at a Welcome Center topped by a 64-foot observation tower built so visitors could look out over the still-mostly-empty canals and woods. The first nine holes of Palm Harbor Golf Course opened in 1971, and the Palm Coast Yacht Club followed in 1972. The first residents, sometimes still called Palm Coast's Pioneers, moved into homes on Club House Drive and Casper Drive in January 1972. Because of that timeline, the handful of original sections built out closest to those first years, generally in the C-Section area, carry the closest thing Palm Coast has to a mature, established feel: older tree canopy and decades of continuous occupancy that newer sections, built out through the 1990s, 2000s, and today, simply have not had time to develop yet.

A private government runs Palm Coast for 26 years, then leaves

For more than two decades, ITT was not just Palm Coast's developer, it functioned as the community's de facto government. The corporation built and maintained roads, water and sewer utilities, parks, and golf courses, and it substantially funded major regional infrastructure, including the Hammock Dunes Bridge in 1988 and the I-95 interchange at Palm Coast Parkway in 1991. That unusual private-government arrangement lasted from 1969 until 1995, when ITT, restructuring its broader business and divesting community-development operations nationally, formally withdrew. That left Palm Coast, still an unincorporated part of Flagler County at the time, with a real governance vacuum: a community of tens of thousands of people with no city government of its own to run it.

Voters build a city of their own: incorporation, December 31, 1999

A Home Rule Coalition, led by retired educator Jim Canfield, pushed to answer that governance vacuum by incorporating Palm Coast as its own city. The referendum was originally scheduled for September 14, 1999, but Hurricane Floyd forced a one-week postponement. When residents finally voted on September 21, 1999, 65.6 percent of the nearly 12,000 ballots cast were in favor, and the City of Palm Coast officially came into existence on December 31, 1999, the last day of the millennium. Jim Canfield became the city's first mayor, and Dick Kelton was hired as its first city manager on April 17, 2000. Measured against the community's own thirty-year head start under ITT, Palm Coast's institutional life as an actual city, with its own police department, comprehensive plan, and city hall, is barely a quarter-century old, and that is genuinely one of the youngest municipal governments in this part of Florida.

Why there is so little historic district here, and what that means for a buyer conversation

This is the part of Palm Coast's history that should change how an agent actually works the town. DeLand has its Garden District bungalows and a downtown protected as a historic district since 1987. Flagler Beach has a century-old pier and buildings the town deliberately chose to preserve. Palm Coast has almost none of that, not because nothing was preserved, but because there was almost nothing old to preserve. The city's built stock is functionally all post-1972, and the overwhelming majority of it is post-1990s. There is no equivalent historic downtown, because ITT platted a suburban grid, not a town center, and the corporate, planned nature of the development means nearly every section sits inside a homeowners association, layered on top of a city land development code that independently mirrors many of those same HOA-style design rules, right down to restrictions like no fences in front yards. So the conversation an agent has with a Palm Coast buyer is fundamentally different from the one in an older town. It is not “what year was this built and who lived here first.” It is which section and HOA the lot sits in, whether it is on a freshwater canal, a saltwater canal, or an interior lot, and whether the property is new construction, a resale from the 1990s or 2000s building boom, or one of the comparatively few original ITT-platted lots that still sits unbuilt more than fifty years after it was first sold. That is a genuinely different skill set than the one an agent needs in DeLand or Flagler Beach, and it is a direct, practical consequence of how this city was built.

Palm Coast today, by the numbers

Palm Coast's growth has been steady and, at times, remarkably fast for a city this young. The 2000 Census counted 32,732 residents, the 2010 Census counted 75,180, and the 2020 Census counted 89,258, with the U.S. Census Bureau's 2024 estimate putting the population at roughly 106,700. Palm Coast has repeatedly ranked among the nation's fastest-growing cities in recent years, including a 2025-2026 U.S. News and World Report ranking placing it 24th nationally by net migration. The median age was 51.6 as of the 2020 Census, notably older than a college town like DeLand, reflecting Palm Coast's mix of retirees and working-age families rather than a young, transient population. Median household income was roughly $77,350 as of 2024. The population is about 69.6 percent White, 10.4 percent Black, and 12.3 percent Hispanic or Latino, per the 2020 Census. None of this is trivia. A city that has more than tripled in population since 2000, still growing this quickly, is a genuinely different market to work than a town whose growth is measured in single digits.

What there actually is to do in Palm Coast

Palm Coast's own recreational identity is built around the same infrastructure ITT laid down decades ago. The city maintains more than 130 miles of trails connecting parks like Belle Terre Park and Ralph Carter Park, and the more than 70 combined miles of freshwater and saltwater canals function as the city's primary boating and waterfront amenity, not a backdrop but the actual recreational core of the place. European Village, an open-air plaza built around a small collection of restaurants and shops, including the Village Pub and Deli, Mezzaluna Pizzeria, La Piazza Cafe, and Osteria Rustica, hosts a weekly Sunday farmers market and is the closest thing Palm Coast has to a walkable town center. Just outside the city itself, Princess Place Preserve, the site of that 1887 hunting lodge, and Washington Oaks Gardens State Park, a 425-acre park with one of the largest outcroppings of coquina rock on the Atlantic coast, both offer the older Florida landscape the platted city grid replaced. For an agent, that combination, modern trail and canal infrastructure inside the city plus real, older Florida parkland just outside it, is a genuine lifestyle selling point distinct from either a historic downtown or an old beach town.

Who's associated with Palm Coast

Because the city is so young, its list of notable residents is short and mostly recent rather than historic. Professional tennis player Reilly Opelka is associated with Palm Coast, as is NASCAR driver Ray Black Jr. Musicians Eddie Money and Leslie West, formerly of the band Mountain, both had ties to the city later in their careers. None of this rises to the kind of deep, generations-old local mythology a town like DeLand has built around its own hometown figures, and that is simply consistent with a city whose entire existence spans a little more than fifty years.

What is your next step?

Palm Coast's history is short, but it is not thin where it counts. A conglomerate built this city from a business plan, and that origin is the actual, practical reason the market here works the way it does: the grid, the canals, the HOA-heavy ownership structure, and a buyer conversation centered on lot type and construction date rather than architectural pedigree.
