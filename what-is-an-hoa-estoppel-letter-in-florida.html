What Is an HOA Estoppel Letter, and Why Does It Matter in Florida?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›HOA Estoppel Letter

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

An HOA estoppel letter, also called an estoppel certificate, is a document issued by a homeowners association or condo association confirming the exact financial status of an account: assessments paid through, any past-due balance, pending special assessments, and transfer or capitalization fees due at closing. Florida law requires one before most HOA or condo sales can close, and the association has 10 business days to deliver it once requested. Fees are capped by statute, currently up to $299 for a standard request, plus up to $119 for expedited delivery and up to $179 more if the account is delinquent. New agents who wait to request it until days before closing are the ones who end up delaying their own deal.

Key takeaways
  • An estoppel certificate confirms what's actually owed on an HOA or condo account: assessments paid through, any delinquency, pending special assessments, and transfer fees due at closing, not just whether dues are current.
  • Florida associations must deliver an estoppel certificate within 10 business days of a written or electronic request. Missing that deadline means the association forfeits the right to charge for it.
  • Fees are capped by statute. As of the 2022 inflation adjustment, the maximum is $299 for a standard request, up to $119 more for expedited delivery within 3 business days, and up to $179 more if the account is delinquent.
  • The certificate is only valid for a set window once issued, 30 days if delivered electronically or 35 days if mailed, so ordering it too early can mean ordering it twice.
  • New agents should request the estoppel the moment a contract goes effective, not the week of closing, since HOA and management company turnaround times vary and a late request is one of the easiest ways to blow a closing date.

What is an HOA estoppel letter?

An estoppel letter, more formally an estoppel certificate, is a document issued by a homeowners association, condo association, or their management company that states the exact financial status of a specific property's account as of a certain date. It confirms whether assessments are paid current, what the regular assessment amount is, whether any special assessment has been approved or is pending, whether there's a delinquent balance, and what fees (like a capital contribution or transfer fee) the buyer or seller will owe the association at closing. It's not a general statement that "the account is fine." It's an itemized, dated snapshot that a title company or closing agent relies on to make sure the association gets paid what it's owed out of the closing proceeds, and that the buyer isn't inheriting a debt they didn't know about.

Why Florida requires this before closing

Almost every HOA or condo sale in Florida needs one because association assessments and any related liens run with the property, not the person. If a seller owes back dues or a special assessment when the property changes hands, that obligation doesn't just disappear at closing, and in many associations it can become a lien against the property itself. The estoppel certificate is how a title company confirms, in writing and from the association directly, exactly what has to be paid off at closing so the new owner starts with a clean account. Florida Statutes 720.30851 (homeowners associations) and 718.116 (condominiums) govern the request process, the required contents, the delivery deadline, and the maximum fees an association can charge, so this isn't a courtesy the association is choosing to provide. It's a legal requirement with real deadlines attached.

What the estoppel certificate actually has to include

Under Florida law, the certificate has to state specifics, not vague assurances. That includes the date it was prepared, the regular periodic assessment amount and when it's next due, the amount of any assessment currently due and unpaid, any special assessment that's been approved but not yet due, any other fees or charges owed to the association, the total amount owed as of a specific date, and contact information for the person who prepared it in case a question comes up. If the association is aware of any pending or anticipated litigation, or a violation of the association's rules affecting the parcel, that has to be disclosed too. This is why an estoppel is worth more than just asking someone at the HOA office whether an account is current. It's a legally binding, itemized statement the association has to stand behind.

What it actually costs in Florida

Florida caps what an association can charge for an estoppel certificate, and the cap has changed over time. The original statutory maximum, set when the estoppel statute was substantially rewritten in 2017, was $250 for a standard request, $100 more for an expedited request, and $150 more if the account was delinquent. Florida law also requires the Department of Business and Professional Regulation to adjust that cap for inflation using the Consumer Price Index, and DBPR issued its first adjustment in 2022. The current maximum, in effect through the next scheduled adjustment, is $299 for a standard request, up to an additional $119 if the certificate is requested on an expedited basis and delivered within 3 business days, and up to an additional $179 if the account is delinquent. Stack all three and the absolute ceiling for a rushed, delinquent-account estoppel is $597, though most requests on a current account cost far less, since the delinquency and expedited fees only apply when they actually apply.

How long the association has to deliver it

The association has 10 business days after receiving a written or electronic request to issue the estoppel certificate. If a party is willing to pay the expedited fee, the association can be asked to deliver it within 3 business days instead. If the association misses the 10-business-day deadline, the statute is direct about the consequence: no fee can be charged for that certificate at all. The obligation to produce it doesn't go away, but the right to get paid for it does. In practice, most well-run management companies deliver well within the window, but self-managed HOAs and understaffed management companies can and do run close to the deadline, which is exactly why a new agent shouldn't assume it'll show up quickly just because the law allows for a fast turnaround.

Why this can delay a closing if you wait

Here's the part that actually matters day to day: an estoppel certificate isn't valid forever once it's issued. It's only good for 30 days if delivered electronically, or 35 days if delivered by mail. That window has to cover the actual closing date, or the title company will need a new one. Agents who wait until the week of closing to request it are cutting it close on two fronts at once, the association's 10-business-day delivery window and the certificate's own 30-to-35-day shelf life. If a title company discovers late in the process that no estoppel has been ordered, or that an old one has expired, the closing date can move, sometimes by weeks if the HOA is slow to respond. The fix is simple and doesn't cost anything extra: order the estoppel the moment a contract goes effective on any HOA or condo property, not the week before the scheduled closing.

What a new agent should actually do with this

A new agent doesn't need to memorize every subsection of the statute, but should know three things cold. First, any HOA or condo sale needs an estoppel certificate, and it's usually ordered by the title company or closing attorney, not the agent personally, but the agent should confirm it's been requested and isn't sitting forgotten. Second, the fee is capped by law, so if an association or management company tries to charge more than the statutory maximum, that's worth flagging to the closing agent. Third, timing is everything: request it early, track the delivery deadline, and make sure whatever certificate lands in the file is still valid on the actual closing date. Buyers and sellers rarely know any of this exists until it becomes a problem. An agent who understands it ahead of time is the one who keeps the deal on schedule.
