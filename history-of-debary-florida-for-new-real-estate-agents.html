The History of DeBary, Florida, for New Real Estate Agents

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›The History of DeBary

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

DeBary takes its name from Samuel Frederick deBary, a wealthy German-born wine and champagne merchant, an importer for Mumm's, who bought 400 acres on the north shore of Lake Monroe in 1871 and built DeBary Hall as his winter hunting lodge. The house hosted presidents and European royalty, anchored a steamboat empire that once ran 13 vessels up the St. Johns River, and outlived the entire deBary family, whose last American heir died in a 1941 plane crash with no children. The city itself did not incorporate until 1993, more than a century later, and its most distinctive modern feature, a SunRail commuter rail station that opened in 2014, sits a short walk from that same 1871 mansion. That combination, a genuine 19th-century estate now run as a public museum next to a 21st-century commuter rail stop, is the real, specific reason DeBary reads differently than its neighbors.

Key takeaways
  • DeBary is named for Samuel Frederick deBary, a wine and champagne importer for Mumm's who bought 400 acres on Lake Monroe in 1871 and built DeBary Hall as a winter hunting lodge.
  • DeBary Hall hosted Presidents Ulysses S. Grant and Grover Cleveland, General Sherman, and European nobility, and deBary's steamboat line once ran 13 vessels between Jacksonville and Enterprise.
  • The deBary family line in America ended abruptly in 1941, when the last heir, Leonie deBary Lyon Brewster, died in a plane crash at 31 with no children, and the estate sat in private and then public hands for decades before Volusia County restored it as a museum.
  • DeBary did not incorporate as a city until December 31, 1993, making it one of the newest municipalities in Volusia County despite its 1871 founding story.
  • DeBary's SunRail commuter rail station opened May 1, 2014, and was the line's northern terminus until it was extended to DeLand in 2024, a genuinely distinctive, current amenity most Volusia towns don't have.

Before deBary: the Timucua and the Arnetts

Long before the name DeBary existed, the north shore of Lake Monroe, where the city sits today, was Timucua territory. Chief Utina's domain reportedly extended north from this area to Lake George, and the wider St. Johns River valley was a significant corridor for Mocama-speaking Timucua populations for centuries before European contact. By around 1760, Seminole peoples migrating from Georgia and Alabama had largely replaced the Timucua in the region. In 1866, a settler named Elijah Watson sold land on the river's north shore to Oliver and Amanda Arnett, and it was the Arnetts who, five years later, sold 400 acres of that land to the man the city would eventually be named for. An agent working DeBary today is selling land with a long human history that predates the 1871 founding story by centuries, not just a blank lot a wealthy New Yorker happened to buy.

A champagne merchant builds a hunting lodge

Samuel Frederick deBary was a German-born wine and champagne merchant based in New York City, an importer connected to Mumm's Champagne, one of the most recognized names in the business. In 1871, he purchased 400 acres from the Arnetts on the north shore of Lake Monroe, near the existing river settlement of Enterprise, and that same year completed a cypress-lumber hunting lodge he intended purely as a winter retreat from New York. He wasn't a land speculator building a town on spec, and he wasn't a farmer chasing citrus riches first. He was a wealthy man who wanted a serious winter estate on the St. Johns River, and DeBary Hall is what he built to have one.

A house built to impress

DeBary Hall was no simple cabin. The finished house ran to roughly 8,000 square feet across 20 rooms in a Southern Colonial and Italianate style, with a two-tiered wraparound veranda, an elevator for guests' luggage, running water fed by a 500-gallon tank in the ceiling, onsite carbonate-gas electricity, a wired call system reaching every room, and a lightning protection system, genuinely advanced features for rural Florida in 1871. The property is also frequently credited with the first swimming pool in Florida, spring-fed and built on the grounds, though a competing claim out of St. Augustine means an agent should present that one as a real but contested distinction rather than settled fact. deBary kept expanding the house over the following decades, adding a second dining room, extra bathrooms, and more guest bedrooms, all built to host the volume of visitors his winters on the St. Johns actually drew. DeBary Hall is the oldest intact building in southwest Volusia County today, and it earned a spot on the National Register of Historic Places on July 24, 1972.

The guest list: presidents, generals, and European nobility

deBary's hospitality wasn't a local reputation, it was a national one. DeBary Hall reportedly hosted Presidents Ulysses S. Grant and Grover Cleveland, General William Tecumseh Sherman, and members of the Astor, Gould, and Vanderbilt families, along with the Prince of Wales, who later became King Edward VII of England. That guest list is a real, documented part of the historical record, not local legend, and it says something specific about the town's earliest identity: DeBary's founding era was defined by serious old-money hospitality on the St. Johns River, not tourism or agriculture in the way most of central Florida developed.

Steamboats, oranges, and an actual business empire

deBary didn't stop at building a house. In 1875, he bought a steamboat for hunting expeditions and hauling fruit, and by 1876 he had formed the DeBary Merchants' Line, which won a federal contract to carry mail between Jacksonville and Enterprise. In 1883, that company merged with Colonel H.T. Baya's line to form the DeBary-Baya Merchants' Line, which at its peak operated 13 steamboats and employed roughly 3,000 people, a genuinely large regional business built on the St. Johns River trade. The Clyde Line bought the operation in 1889, and steamboat service on that route continued until 1928, when road and rail transportation finally made it obsolete. Alongside the steamboats, deBary acquired roughly 9,000 additional acres for orange groves and pecan trees, including land at the Old Enterprise shell midden, an ancient site tied to earlier Indigenous habitation of the area. For an agent, the throughline matters: DeBary wasn't built around a single industry that could collapse overnight, the way a citrus freeze nearly destroyed neighboring DeLand. It was built around one wealthy man's diversified winter estate, and that difference in origin still separates DeBary's character from towns founded purely on agriculture or rail speculation.

The family after Frederick: inheritance and repeated tragedy

Frederick deBary died of a stroke in December 1898. His son Adolphe inherited the business and DeBary Hall, while his daughter Eugenie moved to Germany. Adolphe's own life was marked by loss: all four of his children reportedly predeceased him before his own death in 1928. That left deBary's grandchildren, Leonie and Adolphe Lyon, as the estate's next heirs. Adolphe Lyon, a 1935 Harvard graduate, died on September 19, 1937, at age 23, struck by a car after pushing a young woman out of its path, leaving his sister Leonie the sole heir to DeBary Hall.

The last American deBary

Leonie deBary Lyon married Benjamin Brewster and continued modernizing the estate, adding a tennis court and, notably, a private airstrip and hangar. On May 9, 1941, Leonie and her husband died together in a plane crash after reportedly ignoring storm warnings before takeoff. She was 31 and left no children, ending the deBary family's American line entirely. It's a genuinely sobering piece of the property's history, and it's the real reason DeBary Hall passed out of the family that built it rather than staying a private estate handed down for generations, the way some Florida landmarks did.

From abandoned estate to public museum

The deBary family's European relatives showed little interest in the Florida property, and the estate was sold to V.F. Proctor's Paco Land Company. It changed hands again in 1945, purchased by George Stedronsky of Mount Dora, who moved in the following year and became the first resident of DeBary Hall not related to the deBary family. The house deteriorated over the following decades under a series of owners and leaseholders. The State of Florida purchased the property in 1967 and leased it to various organizations, but it kept declining for roughly 20 more years before Volusia County stepped in to lease and restore it. That restoration is why DeBary Hall functions today as a genuine museum and historic learning center rather than a private ruin or a demolished lot, and it followed the same pattern of deliberate, human decision-making that saved historic buildings in other Volusia towns: nothing about this survival was automatic.

A city more than a century in the making

Despite deBary buying his land in 1871, the town that grew up around DeBary Hall did not become its own incorporated city for another 122 years. DeBary officially incorporated on December 31, 1993, by a vote of roughly 3 to 1 in favor, with a population at the time of about 10,425 residents. That's a genuinely unusual timeline for an agent to understand: DeBary's founding-era identity is 19th century, but its actual municipal government, zoning authority, and city services are recent by comparison, younger than most of the buyers walking into a DeBary listing today.

SunRail arrives: a genuinely current fact, not history

The single most useful modern fact about DeBary for a real estate agent isn't historical at all. The DeBary SunRail station opened May 1, 2014, following a groundbreaking ceremony on June 11, 2012, and it served as the northern terminus of the entire SunRail commuter rail line until the system's Phase 2 North extension reached DeLand's Amtrak station in August 2024. That means DeBary has had a working commuter rail connection into downtown Orlando and the broader Orlando job market for over a decade, a genuinely distinctive amenity most of Volusia County simply doesn't have. For a buyer weighing DeBary against a comparable inland town, that rail access is a real, current, and verifiable selling point, not a historical footnote, and it sits within walking distance of the same 1871 mansion Frederick deBary built as his winter retreat.

DeBary today, by the numbers

DeBary's population has grown steadily and substantially since incorporation: about 7,176 residents in 1990 before the city formed, 15,559 by 2000, 19,320 by 2010, 22,260 in the 2020 census, and an estimated 23,544 more recently. The median age runs around 49.8, older than a typical young-family suburb but not a retirement town either. The population is roughly 77.4% White, 14.6% Hispanic or Latino, 5.0% Black, and 2.3% Asian. Median household income sits around $88,569, notably higher than both the broader Deltona-Daytona Beach-Ormond Beach metro area and the Florida state average, with a comparatively low poverty rate near 5.3%. None of this is trivia for an agent: it points to a market with real, above-average household income and a mature, established population rather than a boom-town of first-time buyers.

The St. Johns River and trail identity DeBary actually earned

DeBary sits on the northern shore of the St. Johns River and genuinely markets itself around that geography rather than around a beach it doesn't have. Gemini Springs Park, at 212 acres, is the largest park in all of Volusia County, built around two natural springs producing roughly 6.5 million gallons of freshwater daily. River City Nature Park adds 113 acres of undeveloped river frontage with a 36-hole disc golf course. DeBary was also named one of Florida's official Trail Towns, sitting at the junction of the St. Johns River-to-Sea Loop, the Coast-to-Coast Trail, and the Heart of Florida Loop, more than 600 combined miles of connected paved trail, with the Spring-to-Spring Trail running directly through town and easily reached from the SunRail station itself. DeBary Hall serves double duty here too, functioning as both the historic estate covered above and the official visitor center and trailhead for that same Spring-to-Spring Trail. For an agent, this is a real, current lifestyle amenity, not marketing language: a genuinely large county park, real river access, and a documented state trail-town designation.

What DeBary actually built as a neighborhood market

DeBary's neighborhoods reflect its layered history more than a single planned development would. DeBary Plantation and Glen Abbey are established golf-course communities, DeBary Plantation dating to 1991 and Glen Abbey's course opening back in 1974. Riviera Bella is a newer gated riverfront community directly on the St. Johns River. Lake Marie Estates and Plantation Estates are older, more affordable neighborhoods dating to the early 1950s, closer in spirit to the town's pre-incorporation decades than to its recent growth. An agent who can explain that DeBary isn't one uniform market, but several genuinely distinct ones layered on top of a 19th-century estate town, is offering real expertise rather than a single median price.

Where DeBary eats

DeBary's dining scene is modest but genuinely local rather than built around chains. La Strega Bistro, an Italian restaurant on US 17-92 with a wood-fired oven and a dedicated wine room, has built a real local following. Nonna Maria's Pizzeria offers a more casual, family-style take on Italian food, and Caribbean Eatz Restaurant & Grill and Pepe's Cantina round out the town's mix of Caribbean and Mexican options. It's not a destination food scene the way downtown DeLand or Flagler Beach can claim, and an honest agent should say so, but it's a real, functioning local restaurant base rather than an empty stretch of highway.

Notable people and what DeBary doesn't claim

DeBary's most notable modern resident by birth is NASA astronaut Luke Delaney, born in 1979. Unlike DeLand's baseball Hall of Famers or Flagler Beach's surfing legends, DeBary doesn't lean heavily on hometown celebrity as part of its identity, and an agent shouldn't oversell what isn't there. The town's real distinguishing story has always been the deBary family's estate and, more recently, its commuter rail access, not a roster of famous natives.

Why this history is a real selling tool, not trivia

A buyer touring DeBary Hall on a Saturday, or choosing a home near the SunRail station specifically for the Orlando commute, is responding to two very different eras of the same small piece of land on Lake Monroe: a 19th-century champagne merchant's winter estate and a 21st-century commuter rail investment. An agent who can explain both, why the mansion exists and who built it, and why the rail station matters for daily life today, is offering something a generic listing sheet never can: a real, honest answer to why this specific spot on the St. Johns River looks and functions the way it does. That's a genuine, durable edge with buyers who are comparing DeBary against a half dozen other Volusia County towns and trying to understand what actually makes it different.
