What Do You Actually Do at Your First Showing?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Your First Showing

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Far less talking than you expect. You arrive early, get in, turn on the lights and open the blinds, then let people walk through their own way while you stay out of the doorway they want to use. Your job is answering factual questions, noticing what they react to rather than what they say, and keeping track of the practical things they will not think about until later. The preparation matters more than the performance, and the single most important skill is knowing which questions you must not answer at all.

Key takeaways
  • Arrive fifteen minutes early. Access is the thing most likely to go wrong and it always goes wrong first.
  • Let them lead. Filling silence while somebody is imagining living there is the most common new-agent mistake.
  • Watch reactions rather than listening to words. People say the kitchen; they linger in the garden.
  • Some questions cannot be answered at all, including whether an area is safe or good. That is a fair housing line.
  • The debrief in the car is where the real information is, and it is worth writing down before you drive off.

Nobody teaches this properly. Pre-licensing covers law and contracts and says almost nothing about the ordinary experience of standing in somebody else's kitchen while two people decide whether they could live there.

Before you go: the part that actually matters

Confirm access, then confirm it again. Access is the single thing most likely to fail, and it fails at the worst possible moment. Know whether it is a lockbox, a code, a key at the office, or an appointment where somebody lets you in. If it is a lockbox, know your key works and is not about to be suspended over an expired card, which is one of the practical traps in how you get an MLS lockbox key here.

Read the listing properly. Year built, roof age, square footage, taxes, association fees and rules, and anything unusual in the remarks. You will be asked, and answering with a number rather than a promise to check is most of what credibility looks like early on.

Drive the route. Especially with more than one property. Knowing the order, the parking, the gate arrangements and how long the drive between two of them takes prevents the whole appointment feeling improvised.

Know what you cannot answer. More on this below, and it is the part to settle before you are asked rather than during.

On arrival

Get there ten or fifteen minutes early. Open the front door, turn on the lights, open blinds, and take a quick walk through so nothing surprises you in front of the client. If a previous visitor left the air conditioning off in August, you want to know that before your buyers walk into it.

If the seller is home, which happens, introduce yourself briefly and then keep the interaction short. Buyers do not speak freely in front of an owner, and a seller lingering nearby is the fastest way to a quiet, uninformative showing.

Inside: say less than feels natural

This is the mistake almost every new agent makes. Silence feels like failure, so they narrate: here is the kitchen, note the countertops, this room has good light. The buyer already knows it is a kitchen.

Let them lead. Walk in, say something orienting like the primary bedroom is at the back and the garage is through there, then get out of the way. Stand somewhere that is not the doorway people want to use. Let them open cupboards and closets. When a couple starts talking to each other about furniture, that is the sound of a good showing, and interrupting it is expensive.

Answer questions factually when they come. If you do not know, say so and find out, which is a great deal better than guessing, per what to say when a client asks something you do not know.

And watch. People will tell you the kitchen is the priority and then spend six minutes in the garden. What they respond to physically is more reliable than what they say they want, and it is the information that makes your next set of choices better.

The questions you must not answer

This deserves its own section because it is the one place where a friendly instinct creates real risk.

Buyers routinely ask whether a neighborhood is safe, whether it is a good area, whether the schools are any good, or what kind of people live there. Those questions cannot be answered by a licensee, because characterizing an area in those terms risks steering and a fair housing violation regardless of what you intended.

The correct response is to provide sources rather than conclusions: point them to published crime statistics, school district information and demographic data, and let them draw their own conclusions. Say plainly that you are not permitted to characterize an area and that you will help them find the information. Most clients accept that immediately when it is explained rather than dodged.

Two backgrounds find this hardest, for opposite reasons: first responders, who may genuinely know the answer, and social workers, who are trained to be helpful with exactly this kind of question.

Things worth noticing while you are there

You are not an inspector and should not act like one, a line covered in the transition from the trades. But observation is part of the job, and in Florida a few things are worth registering because they affect whether a deal will work at all.

Roof age and condition, because insurability often turns on it. Signs of water intrusion or previous repair. Whether the property sits in a flood zone, per flood zones a Volusia and Flagler agent should understand. Association rules that would rule the property out for this buyer, such as restrictions on pets, vehicles or rentals.

Note them, raise them as things to look into, and route anything structural to a licensed inspector. The insurance dimension is covered in what a four point inspection is and wind mitigation inspections.

On the way out, and afterwards

Leave the house exactly as you found it. Lights off if they were off, blinds as they were, doors locked, lockbox properly closed. This is a small thing that other agents notice, and being the person whose showings never generate a complaint is worth more than it sounds in a market this size.

Then debrief while it is fresh, ideally in the car. Ask what they liked, what they would change, and how it compared to the last one. The most useful question is usually the simplest: could you live here? People answer that more honestly than they answer questions about features.

Write it down before you drive off. In three weeks and eleven properties you will not remember which house had the garden they liked, and a client noticing that you remembered is a large part of why people refer their agent.

What good looks like on your first one

You got in without a problem, you did not oversell, you answered what you could and admitted what you could not, you avoided the questions that are not yours to answer, and you left with a clearer sense of what this buyer actually wants than you arrived with.

Nervousness is fine and largely invisible. Buyers are focused on the house, not on evaluating your performance, and the ones who become clients do so because you were useful and straight with them rather than because you were polished.

The honest bottom line

Prepare properly, arrive early, open the house up, and then talk considerably less than instinct suggests. Answer facts, refuse the questions that carry fair housing risk, notice what people actually respond to, and capture it before it fades.

Do that and your second showing will be materially better than your first, which is the only thing the first one is really for.
