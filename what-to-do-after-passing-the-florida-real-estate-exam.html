What to Do After Passing the Florida Real Estate Exam

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›After Passing the Exam

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Passing the Florida real estate exam gets you a license, but it’s issued inactive. Before you can legally practice, you need to choose a sponsoring broker and activate your license under them with the DBPR. From there, decide whether to join the local Realtor board and MLS, get your basic business tools set up, and mark your calendar: passing starts the clock on your 45 hours of required post-licensing education.

Key takeaways
  • Your Florida license is issued inactive after you pass; you can’t legally practice until it’s activated under a sponsoring broker.
  • Choosing a sponsoring broker is the very next step, and usually the brokerage handles submitting the activation paperwork to DBPR.
  • Joining the local Realtor board and MLS is a separate decision, generally needed only if you plan to actively list and show property.
  • Passing the exam starts the clock on your 45-hour post-licensing course, due before your first license renewal.
  • Getting basic tools in place (a work email, a simple CRM, business cards) before your first client conversation saves scrambling later.

Passing the exam is a milestone, not a finish line

Passing the Florida real estate exam is genuinely hard, and it’s worth pausing to feel good about it. But the license you’re issued right after passing isn’t ready to use yet. The Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) issues new sales associates an inactive license. Inactive means exactly what it sounds like: you’re licensed, but you can’t legally represent a buyer or seller, market a listing, or earn a commission until that license is activated under a supervising broker.

Choose a sponsoring broker

This is the real next step, and arguably the most important decision of your early career. In Florida, every sales associate must practice under a licensed broker who takes on legal responsibility for your work. That broker is where you’ll get your training, your day-to-day support, and often your first leads. Don’t rush this. Talk to more than one office, ask what the first 90 days actually look like for a brand-new agent, and pay attention to whether the people there seem genuinely willing to answer a beginner’s questions rather than handing you a stack of forms and wishing you luck.

It also helps to ask about the practical side of joining: what fees apply, whether errors and omissions insurance is provided through the brokerage, and who you'd actually go to on day one with a question about your first contract. Those answers tell you as much about a brokerage as any pitch does.

Get your license activated with DBPR

Once you’ve chosen a broker, activating your license is mostly a paperwork step, and most brokerages walk you through it or handle the submission on your behalf through the state’s online licensing system. Until that activation is processed and your license status shows as active, you still can’t practice, even if you’ve already agreed to join a brokerage. It’s worth confirming your status directly through your DBPR account rather than assuming it’s done, since a delay here quietly pushes back everything after it.

Mark your 45-hour post-licensing deadline

Here’s the step new agents most often overlook: passing the exam and activating your license starts the clock on a separate requirement, 45 hours of post-licensing education, due before your very first license renewal. This is not the same as the ongoing continuing education you’ll complete every renewal cycle after that; it’s a one-time requirement tied specifically to your first term as a new licensee. Miss the deadline, and your license can lapse. Put the date somewhere you’ll actually see it, not just in the back of your mind.

Decide whether to join the local Realtor board and MLS

Joining your local Realtor board and the MLS is a separate decision from activating your license, and it comes with its own dues. If you’re planning to actively list and show homes, you’ll want access to the MLS fairly quickly since it’s the backbone of how listings get shared and shown across a market. If you’re starting in a more limited or referral-focused capacity, this step can wait. Your sponsoring broker can walk you through what's actually needed for the kind of practice you're planning.

Set up the basics before your first conversation

Before you take your first client call, get a few unglamorous things in place: a professional email address, a simple way to track contacts and follow-ups (even a basic spreadsheet works at the start, though most agents move to a CRM fairly quickly), and business cards. None of this needs to be elaborate. The goal is just to look and feel ready the first time someone asks “can you help me find a house,” instead of scrambling to figure out how you'll actually stay in touch with them.

This is also a good moment to think through the practical side of income while you get going: real estate is commission-based, so there’s often a gap between your first activated month and your first closed check. New agents who plan around that gap, rather than being surprised by it, tend to feel far less pressure during their first few months.

Lean on your broker for the sequence, not just the paperwork

The order of these steps can shift slightly depending on your broker and your local board’s process, so the single best move after passing is to ask your sponsoring broker directly: what do I need to do this week, this month, and before my post-licensing deadline. A good broker will already have this mapped out for new agents, because they've walked new licensees through it before, and they can tell you which of these steps their office handles for you versus which ones are on you.
