Basic Home Staging Tips Every Listing Agent Should Know

Home›For Experienced Agents›Home Staging Tips

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Before a listing goes live, a good agent walks the seller through decluttering and depersonalizing, opening up natural light, fixing small cosmetic flaws, and arranging furniture so rooms photograph and show larger. These basics apply to every listing, from a starter home to a high-end property, and cost little compared with what a listing sells for.

Key takeaways
  • Decluttering and depersonalizing helps buyers picture themselves living in the home instead of seeing the current owner's life.
  • Natural light and clean, bright rooms consistently photograph and show better than dim, cluttered ones.
  • Small cosmetic fixes (scuffed paint, loose handles, burnt-out bulbs) are cheap relative to the impact they have on first impressions.
  • Furniture arrangement that opens up walking paths and removes oversized pieces makes rooms read as bigger than they are.
  • Full professional staging services exist for vacant or higher-end listings, but these basics apply to every listing regardless of price point.

Why staging matters before the photos ever get taken

Most buyers start their search online, scrolling through listing photos long before they ever request a showing. That means the way a home presents in photos, and then in person, often determines how many showings a listing generates in the first place. A listing agent who walks a seller through a few basic staging steps before the photographer arrives is protecting the marketing investment already being made in professional photography, not adding extra work for its own sake.

Start with decluttering and depersonalizing

The single highest-impact, lowest-cost step is clearing out excess belongings. Family photos, personal collections, and an overflowing closet make it harder for a buyer to picture their own life in the space. Encourage sellers to box up personal items, thin out furniture, and clear countertops so rooms feel open rather than lived-in. A temporarily rented storage unit or a well-organized garage is often money well spent for the few weeks a home is on the market.

Let the natural light in

Bright rooms consistently show and photograph better than dim ones. Before every showing and photo session, open blinds and curtains fully, clean the windows, and replace any burnt-out bulbs with a warmer, brighter option. In rooms that stay dark even with the blinds open, an inexpensive floor or table lamp in a corner can make a real difference. This is one of the easiest fixes on this list and one of the most consistently noticeable to a buyer walking through.

Handle small cosmetic fixes before the photographer arrives

This isn't about a full renovation. It's about the small flaws a buyer's eye catches immediately: scuffed paint on a hallway wall, a loose cabinet handle, a dripping faucet, or nail holes left from removed art. A bold accent wall color that photographs poorly is often worth repainting a neutral tone before listing photos. These fixes typically cost a fraction of what they save in a buyer's first impression, and they're worth walking through room by room with the seller ahead of time.

Arrange furniture to make rooms feel bigger

How furniture is arranged changes how large a room reads, in photos and in person. Pull furniture slightly off the walls to create depth, remove any oversized piece that crowds a walkway, and make sure there's a clear path through every room. In open floor plans or awkward bonus rooms, using furniture to clearly define what a space is for (a reading nook, a home office corner) helps a buyer understand how to use it, rather than seeing an ambiguous empty area.

Don't overlook curb appeal

The first impression starts before a buyer ever walks through the front door. A freshly mowed lawn, trimmed bushes, a clean front door and porch area, fresh mulch in the beds, and a pressure-washed driveway are all inexpensive relative to the price of the home, and they set the tone for everything a buyer sees inside. Agents who skip curb appeal are often working against a negative first impression that no amount of interior staging can fully undo, no matter how well the inside of the home is prepared.

Keep small sensory details in mind, too

A few finishing touches often get overlooked but make a noticeable difference. Beds should be made, fresh towels hung in bathrooms, and dishes cleared from the sink before any showing. Strong cooking odors, pet smells, and heavy air fresheners can all work against a listing, so a neutral, lightly aired-out home tends to leave a better impression than one that smells like it's being actively lived in. Bold or dated wall colors are also worth repainting a neutral, widely appealing tone if the budget allows, since neutral walls make it easier for a buyer to picture their own furniture and style in the space.

When basic staging isn't enough

For a vacant home or a higher-end listing, full professional staging services (rented furniture, a professional designer's eye) are often worth the added cost. An empty room is genuinely harder for most buyers to picture furnished, and a vacant listing usually benefits the most from bringing in a stager. Part of an experienced listing agent's value is knowing when the basics above are enough on their own and when it's worth recommending that additional investment, based on the home's price point, condition, and how long it's likely to sit on the market without it.
