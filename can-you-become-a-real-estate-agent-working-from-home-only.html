Can You Become a Real Estate Agent Working From Home Only in Florida?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Remote Work Reality

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Not entirely, and it’s worth hearing that honestly before you enroll in a pre-license course. A real amount of the work, prospecting calls, paperwork, listing prep, marketing, and client communication, can genuinely be done from a home office. But real estate is fundamentally a face-to-face business. Showings, listing appointments, inspections, and open houses require being physically present in the home, and there’s no way to build a real business around this career without that in-person piece.

Key takeaways
  • A real share of agent work, calls, follow-up, paperwork, listing descriptions, and marketing, can be done from home on a laptop and a phone.
  • Showings, listing appointments, and open houses cannot be done remotely; they require walking a physical property with a physical client.
  • Even administrative tasks like the pre-license course itself can be completed online, but the license only becomes useful once you're doing in-person work with clients.
  • Some experienced agents build teams where they handle more calls and coordination while a partner or team member covers showings, but that's a business structure built after establishing a track record, not a starting point.
  • If your goal is a career you can run entirely from a screen with no in-person client contact, real estate sales is genuinely not the right fit; other roles in the industry (transaction coordination, for example) are closer to that.

Can you really work from home as a real estate agent?

Partly, and that’s the honest answer this question deserves. A meaningful share of the day-to-day work of running a real estate business genuinely happens from wherever you have a laptop, a phone, and a decent internet connection: prospecting calls, following up with past clients, writing listing descriptions, building marketing materials, reviewing contracts, and communicating with buyers, sellers, lenders, and title companies. Agents who are organized about their time can legitimately handle a large portion of a normal week from a home office. But that’s only part of the job, and the part that’s missing is the part that actually closes deals.

What genuinely cannot be done remotely

Showings are the clearest example. A buyer needs to physically walk through a home, feel the layout, notice the light, hear the street noise, and stand in the kitchen to know if it’s right for them. That can’t be replaced by a video call or a set of photos, no matter how good the listing marketing is. Listing appointments work the same way; sitting across the table from a seller in their own home, walking the property with them, and talking through pricing and staging face-to-face builds a level of trust that a phone call doesn’t. Open houses require someone physically present in the home for hours at a time. Inspections and final walk-throughs are the same story. None of these can move to a screen without losing the thing that makes them work.

Why this isn’t a technicality, it’s the core of the job

It’s tempting to think of showings and appointments as one task among many on a long list, but they’re not a side task. They’re the actual transaction happening. Real estate is, at its core, a relationship and trust business built around a home, which is a physical thing a client needs to experience in person before making one of the largest financial decisions of their life. An agent who tried to run a full practice without ever being physically present for a showing or a listing appointment wouldn’t have much of a business, regardless of how strong their marketing or phone skills were.

What a realistic remote-heavy week actually looks like

For agents who want to maximize the at-home portion of their week, the honest version looks like this: mornings spent on calls, follow-up, and CRM work from home; midday and afternoon blocks reserved for showings, listing appointments, and open houses out in the field; evenings back home for paperwork, contract review, and preparing for the next day. The ratio shifts depending on how busy your pipeline is. A slower week might genuinely be seventy percent home-based. A week with multiple closings and several active showings will flip that ratio the other way. What doesn’t change is that the in-person piece never goes to zero for an agent actively working with buyers and sellers.

Does technology close the gap over time?

Tools like video calls, 3D virtual tours, and drone footage genuinely help, and it’s worth being fair to them. A well-made virtual tour can help an out-of-town buyer decide whether a home is even worth flying in to see, and video calls can handle a lot of the early conversation and paperwork walkthroughs that used to require an in-person meeting. What none of that technology has replaced, even with steady improvement, is the moment a buyer actually decides to make an offer, or a seller decides to trust an agent with their largest asset. Both of those moments still happen overwhelmingly in person, because the stakes are high enough that people want to be physically present, and want their agent physically present with them, before committing. Technology has shrunk the amount of in-person time required. It hasn’t brought it to zero, and there’s no real evidence it’s heading there.

Why clients still expect an agent who shows up

Buying or selling a home is one of the largest financial decisions most people ever make, and that alone shapes what clients expect from the person guiding them through it. A buyer who can’t get their agent to physically meet them at a showing, or a seller who never gets a face-to-face listing appointment, tends to lose confidence fast, regardless of how responsive that agent is by phone or text. This isn’t really about technology limitations; it’s about what the relationship actually requires to build trust at this scale. Agents who try to run their whole practice at arm’s length usually find that clients quietly move on to someone willing to show up, well before the transaction gets anywhere close to closing.

Can you build a team to cover the in-person part for you?

Some experienced agents eventually structure their business this way, bringing on a showing partner or buyer’s agent who handles more of the property visits while they focus on listings, marketing, and client relationships from a more office-based role. That’s a legitimate model, but it’s something built over time, after establishing a client base and a track record, not something a brand-new agent can set up on day one. Starting out, you’re the one doing the showings, because that’s how you build the experience and relationships that eventually let you delegate any part of it.

What if you focus on listings instead of buyers?

Some agents structure their business to lean more toward listing sellers’ homes rather than working buyer showings, thinking that might reduce the in-person load. It genuinely shifts the mix, but it doesn’t remove the in-person requirement. A listing appointment itself is typically done face-to-face in the seller’s home, walking the property, discussing pricing strategy, and talking through staging and prep, all of which tend to go far better in person than over a call. Once a listing is live, open houses and buyer showings for that property still need someone physically present, whether that’s you or a showing partner. Leaning listing-heavy can genuinely change your schedule’s shape, but it doesn’t turn the job into a fully remote one.

What can actually be done online during licensing

The good news is that getting to the license itself can genuinely be done remotely. Florida’s pre-license coursework is available online and at your own pace, and much of the exam preparation can happen from home too. It’s once you’re licensed and actively working with real buyers and sellers that the in-person requirement becomes unavoidable, since the license only creates value when you’re doing the client-facing work it authorizes.

If you want a role with no in-person client contact

If a fully remote career, with genuinely zero in-person client work, is the actual goal, real estate sales isn’t the right fit, and it’s more useful to know that honestly before enrolling in a course than after. Adjacent roles in the industry, like transaction coordination or working behind the scenes in a brokerage’s marketing or operations, sit closer to that description, since they support transactions without requiring the agent-facing showings and appointments.
