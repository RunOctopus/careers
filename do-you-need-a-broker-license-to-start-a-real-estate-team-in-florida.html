Do You Need a Broker License to Start a Real Estate Team in Florida?

Home›For Experienced Agents›Starting a Team

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

No, you don’t need a Florida broker license to start a real estate team, as long as the team operates under your existing sponsoring broker. Teams work under Chapter 475 as part of the brokerage, not as a separate legal entity, and the sponsoring broker registers the team name with the DBPR. You’d only need an actual broker license if you wanted to open your own independent brokerage instead of a team inside one.

Key takeaways
  • You don’t need a Florida broker license to start or lead a real estate team under your existing sponsoring broker.
  • Teams operate under Chapter 475 as part of the brokerage, not as a separate legal entity with their own license.
  • Your sponsoring broker, not you individually, registers the team or group name with the Florida DBPR.
  • Team and group names can’t include words like Agency, Associates, Brokerage, or Brokers, since those imply a separate licensed company.
  • You only need a broker license if you want to open your own independent brokerage rather than a team inside an existing one.

What counts as a "team" under Florida real estate law?

In Florida, a real estate team is not its own legal entity. Under Chapter 475, Florida Statutes, a team is simply a group of licensees, usually one lead agent and one or more team members, who work together and market themselves as a unit while still operating under the same sponsoring broker. The team has no separate license, no separate trust account, and no independent legal standing. Everything the team does still happens inside the brokerage that holds everyone’s licenses.

Do you need a broker’s license to start a team?

No. You do not need a Florida broker license to start or lead a real estate team, as long as that team operates under your existing sponsoring broker. You can be a sales associate, recruit other agents to join your team, brand yourselves together, and split production and referrals, all without ever taking the broker exam. The broker license requirement only applies if you want to step outside a sponsoring brokerage entirely and run your own independent brokerage instead of a team inside one.

Who registers the team name with the state?

Your supervising broker is the one who registers the team or group name with the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR), not you individually. The team operates as a group or team designation inside the brokerage’s license, and the broker carries responsibility for making sure the name, advertising, and structure meet DBPR rules. This is one more reason the broker you choose to hang your license with matters: they carry the compliance responsibility for how your team is allowed to present itself to the public.

What can’t you call your team?

Florida rules are specific about team naming. A team or group name generally cannot include words implying it is its own brokerage or company, such as Agency, Associates, Brokerage, or Brokers, since those terms could mislead the public into thinking the team is a separately licensed entity. The name also typically needs to include the last name of at least one team member and must be approved and registered through the sponsoring broker. These rules are detailed and periodically updated, so confirm exact current requirements with your broker and the DBPR before finalizing a team name.

When do you actually need a broker license?

You need an actual Florida broker license only if you want to open your own independent brokerage, meaning you want to hold your own trust account, supervise other licensees directly under your own license rather than a sponsoring broker’s, and take on the legal and regulatory responsibility of being broker of record. That is a materially bigger step than building a team: it means qualifying beyond the sales associate level, meeting experience requirements, and carrying ongoing compliance obligations that a team leader inside an established brokerage simply does not carry.

Team leader vs. broker: what actually changes?

Building a team as a sales associate lets you focus on what most agents actually want: growing production, recruiting talent, and building a business around your name, while the brokerage still handles the legal infrastructure, errors and omissions coverage, trust accounting, and DBPR compliance behind you. Becoming a broker means taking on that infrastructure yourself, along with the ongoing continuing-education and reporting obligations that come with holding a broker license rather than a sales associate license. For most agents who want to scale without taking on brokerage-level liability and overhead, starting a team under a strong existing broker gets them almost everything they want, faster and with far less regulatory risk.

Does starting a team change how you get paid?

Not structurally. Commission still flows through the brokerage’s trust account the same way it does for any sales associate, and the brokerage still takes its normal split before team splits are applied among the lead agent and team members. Starting a team is mostly an internal arrangement about how production, leads, and commissions get divided among the people on the team; it does not change the brokerage’s role as the licensed entity handling the transaction and the money.

What should you look for in a broker before starting a team?

Since your team lives inside your sponsoring broker’s structure, the broker’s support matters more than most new team leads expect. Look for a brokerage with real experience registering and supporting teams, clear splits and agreements for team production, and a track record of standing behind agents who want to grow rather than just collect desk fees. The right broker makes starting a team straightforward; the wrong one can create friction over naming, splits, or supervision down the road.
