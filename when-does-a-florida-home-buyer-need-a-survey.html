When Does a Florida Home Buyer Need a Survey?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Surveys in a Florida Home Sale

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

Florida law does not require a survey on every home sale. Whether one gets ordered usually comes down to the lender and the title company, not the state. Most mortgage lenders require a current survey as a condition of funding, and title companies generally want one too, because without it the buyer's title insurance policy carries a survey exception that excludes coverage for boundary disputes, encroachments, and setback violations. A survey shows the property's legal boundaries, easements, and the actual footprint of structures on the lot, which is exactly the information needed to confirm a fence, driveway, addition, or shed doesn't cross a property line or sit inside a restricted setback or easement. A residential survey typically costs $300 to $700, sometimes more depending on lot size and complexity, and takes roughly 5 to 10 business days. A cash buyer waiving a survey is taking on real, if usually small, risk that a financed buyer with a lender-required survey doesn't carry.

Key takeaways
  • Florida does not have a statewide legal requirement for a survey on every real estate sale. It's a practical requirement driven by lenders and title companies, not a legal mandate from the state.
  • Most mortgage lenders require a current survey, generally one completed within the last 60 to 90 days, as a condition of closing, though some will accept an older survey if no changes have been made to the property since.
  • A survey directly affects title insurance. Without one, a title policy typically includes a survey exception that excludes coverage for boundary disputes, encroachments, and setback issues, which a current survey can remove.
  • A survey becomes genuinely important in specific situations: unclear or disputed boundary lines, a fence that may not follow the actual property line, an addition or structure built close to a lot line, or any property where a prior survey doesn't reflect current improvements.
  • A residential survey typically costs $300 to $700 and takes about 5 to 10 business days, and in most Florida transactions the buyer pays for it as part of closing costs.

Does Florida law require a survey on every home sale?

No. There's no Florida statute that requires a property survey as part of every residential real estate closing. In that sense, a survey is legally optional on any given sale. But in practice, surveys show up in the large majority of Florida transactions anyway, because the requirement doesn't come from the state, it comes from the lender financing the purchase and the title company insuring it. Once those two parties are involved, which is true of nearly every financed transaction, a survey effectively becomes a real requirement even though no law forces it.

What a survey actually shows

A property survey is a professional, measured drawing of a specific parcel of land, prepared by a licensed surveyor, that documents the legal boundary lines of the property, any recorded easements crossing it, and the physical location of structures, fences, driveways, and other improvements relative to those boundaries. It answers a set of very concrete questions: exactly where does this property legally end and the neighbor's begin, is there a utility or drainage easement that limits what can be built in part of the yard, does the fence actually sit on the property line or does it drift onto the neighbor's land or leave a strip of the buyer's own land on the wrong side, and does an addition, pool, or shed encroach on a setback line or an easement. None of that is guesswork or visual estimation, it's based on the recorded legal description and actual field measurements, which is exactly why a survey carries real legal weight that a buyer's own walk-through never can.

Why lenders and title companies push for one

Mortgage lenders in Florida commonly require a current survey, generally one completed within roughly the last 60 to 90 days, as a condition of funding the loan, though some lenders will accept an older survey if the seller can confirm no significant improvements or changes have been made to the property since it was done. The title insurance side is where a survey matters even more directly. Without a current survey, a title company generally can't confirm the property doesn't have an undisclosed boundary problem, so the title insurance policy includes what's called a survey exception, language that specifically excludes coverage for boundary disputes, encroachments, and setback violations. That means if a boundary problem surfaces after closing, without a survey exception removed, the buyer's title policy may not cover it. A current survey is what lets the title company remove that exception and extend real coverage to those risks, which is the practical reason most title companies and lenders push for a survey even though Florida law itself doesn't demand one.

Situations where a survey becomes genuinely important

Some transactions make a survey far more than a formality. A fence that's existed for years but was never confirmed against the actual recorded boundary can turn into a real dispute the moment a new owner on either side wants to rebuild it, extend it, or build something new near it, and a survey is what settles that question before it becomes a fight. A home with an addition, a pool enclosure, a shed, or a detached garage built close to a property line needs a survey to confirm those structures don't cross a setback requirement or sit inside a recorded easement, since a structure built in violation of either can create real problems for a future owner, including possible removal requirements. Properties where boundary lines have ever been unclear, disputed, or where a neighbor has raised a concern in the past are an obvious case where skipping a survey is a real gamble rather than a minor convenience. And any property where the most recent survey on file predates significant changes, a new fence, a new addition, a change in landscaping that shifted where a driveway or structure sits, deserves a fresh one rather than relying on outdated paperwork.

What it costs and how long it takes

A typical residential boundary survey in Florida costs roughly $300 to $700, with larger, irregularly shaped, or more complex lots running higher, sometimes up toward $1,000 to $2,000 in unusual cases. Turnaround is generally 5 to 10 business days under normal conditions, though rush service is often available for an additional fee if a closing timeline is tight. In most Florida transactions, the buyer pays for a new survey as part of their closing costs, though this is negotiable between buyer and seller like most other closing cost items.

What happens when a buyer skips it

A cash buyer, or a buyer whose lender happens to accept an older survey, can technically close without ever ordering a new one. Doing so isn't reckless in every case, particularly on a property with a simple, rectangular lot, no additions, no disputed history, and a recent prior survey already on file. But it does mean accepting the survey exception on the title policy, giving up coverage for boundary disputes and encroachments that a current survey would otherwise help remove. On a straightforward property, that's usually a small, manageable risk. On a property with any of the red flags above, an unclear boundary, a structure near a lot line, an older or missing survey, it's a real risk, and an agent who understands the difference is in a much better position to advise a buyer on when paying a few hundred dollars for a survey is worth it and when it genuinely isn't.

The different kinds of surveys a buyer might encounter

Not every survey is the same depth of work, and knowing the difference helps set the right expectation with a buyer or a lender. A boundary survey is the most thorough version, establishing the exact legal property lines through field measurement and research into the recorded plat and legal description, and it's the version most capable of supporting a full removal of a title policy's survey exception. A mortgage or spot survey, sometimes just called a location survey, is a lighter version that many lenders will accept, showing the general location of improvements relative to the boundaries without the same level of precision as a full boundary survey. It's faster and cheaper, but it may not be enough if a genuine boundary question comes up later. An as-built survey, often prepared after new construction or a major addition, confirms exactly where a finished structure actually sits once it's built, which can differ slightly from what the original site plan called for. Knowing which type a lender or title company is actually asking for, rather than assuming any survey will do, avoids a buyer paying for the wrong one and having to order a second.
