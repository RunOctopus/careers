Do You Need a Local Area Code for Your Cell Phone as a Real Estate Agent?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Local Area Code

Updated August 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

No. Nothing in Florida’s real estate licensing law or in FREC’s advertising rule says a licensee’s telephone number has to carry a local area code. You can hold a Florida license, work in Volusia or Flagler County, and answer a number that still says 631 or 216. What the rule does care about is different: under Rule 61J2-10.025, F.A.C., a telephone number is “point of contact information,” so wherever you publish it, the licensed name of your brokerage has to appear with it, and the advertisement cannot be misleading. If you still want a 386 number, a free second line that rings your existing phone solves it in an afternoon without changing carriers.

Key takeaways
  • There is no area code requirement anywhere in Florida real estate licensing law or in the FREC advertising rule.
  • What the rule does regulate is your brokerage’s licensed name appearing with your point of contact information, which explicitly includes telephone numbers.
  • The practical downside of an out-of-area number is narrow: it shows up mostly in cold outreach, where an unfamiliar area code is one more reason a stranger lets the call ring.
  • A free second line lets you pick a number by city or area code and forward it to the phone you already carry, so you do not have to change carriers or numbers.
  • The question that matters more, and almost nobody asks it early: whether the number your clients have is yours or your brokerage’s when you eventually move.

This question comes up in nearly every pre-licensing class and new agent conversation, and it comes up more here than in most parts of Florida, because a large share of the people entering this business in Volusia and Flagler County moved here from somewhere else and are still carrying the number they had in New York, Ohio, or New Jersey. The short answer is that the state does not care. The longer answer is that the state cares about something else entirely, and it is worth knowing which is which before you print business cards.

Is a local area code required anywhere in Florida law?

No. Florida’s licensing requirements cover your age, your education, your background check, the state exam, and your sponsoring broker. There is no field on the application about your telephone number, no rule tying a licensee to a geographic phone prefix, and no continuing education module about it. A licensed Florida agent working out of an Ormond Beach office with a 312 cell number is fully compliant, and no regulator is going to raise it.

That is worth saying plainly, because the question is usually asked as though it were a rule, when it is really a marketing question wearing a rule’s clothing.

What Florida actually regulates about your phone number

There is a real rule about phone numbers, it is just not the one people expect. Rule 61J2-10.025, F.A.C., the FREC advertising rule, defines “point of contact information” as any means by which someone can reach the brokerage or the individual licensee, and it names telephone numbers specifically alongside mailing addresses, street addresses, email addresses, and fax numbers.

Two obligations follow from that. First, wherever you publish a number, the licensed name of your brokerage has to appear with it. On a website, the rule requires the brokerage name to sit adjacent to, immediately above, or immediately below the point of contact information. Second, no advertisement a licensee places can be fraudulent, false, deceptive, or misleading, and all advertising has to be done in a way that a reasonable person would know they are dealing with a real estate licensee.

So the compliance question is not which number you use. It is whether the brokerage name travels with it. An agent who runs a social media profile with a phone number and no brokerage name has an actual rule problem. An agent with a Cleveland area code and the brokerage name properly displayed does not.

Why the question comes up so much here

Volusia and Flagler County absorb a steady stream of people from out of state, and real estate is one of the careers those arrivals most often move into, because it is licensable in months rather than years and it rewards knowing a place you have just chosen to live in. That means an unusually high share of new agents in this market are starting out with a number that identifies them, correctly, as someone who recently arrived.

The worry underneath the question is rarely about phones. It is about whether being new to the area is a disadvantage, and the area code feels like the visible evidence of it. That is a fair concern to have, and it is worth answering directly rather than through the phone number: a lot of buyers here are also from somewhere else, and an agent who moved from the same region a client is moving from is frequently an asset rather than a liability. We wrote about that separately in whether real estate is a good career for someone who just moved to Florida.

Does an out-of-area number actually cost you business?

Honestly, less than people assume, and not zero. It depends almost entirely on where your business comes from.

If your calls are inbound, from a brokerage lead, a portal inquiry, a sign call routed to you, a past client, or a referral from someone you know, the person calling either already knows who you are or is expecting to hear from you. The area code is invisible in those conversations. Most of what a new agent does in year one, building a sphere and working referrals, falls into this category.

Where it does show up is outbound contact with strangers. Cold calls, expired listings, for-sale-by-owner outreach, and follow-up to a lead who has never heard your name are all situations where the recipient is deciding in about two seconds whether to answer an unknown number. An unfamiliar area code is one more small reason to let it ring. It is worth being clear about the size of that effect: unknown numbers go unanswered at a high rate no matter what prefix they carry, so a local area code improves a bad answer rate rather than fixing it.

There is a second, quieter version of the same effect on text messages and voicemails, where a 386 number reads as someone nearby and a distant one reads as a call center. Neither impression is fair, and both are real.

How to get a local number without changing your phone

If you decide you want a 386 line, you do not have to switch carriers, buy a second phone, or give up the number your friends and family use. Free call-forwarding services will let you search available numbers by city or area code, assign you one at no cost, and ring your existing phone whenever someone calls it. Texts to that number arrive in the service’s app rather than your normal messages.

If you would rather move your existing number into that service instead of adding a new one, that is also possible, though it is a paid, one-way step rather than a free one. Moving an active mobile number into one of these services generally carries a one-time fee, commonly around twenty dollars, and the number has to be an eligible mobile line. Do not cancel your carrier plan until the port is confirmed complete, or you risk losing the number in the gap.

For most new agents, the free second number is the right version. It is reversible, it costs nothing, and it lets you keep work and personal calls separable, which becomes valuable faster than you expect in a job where clients text at nine on a Sunday night.

What to check before you route your business through a second number

A second line only helps if you actually answer it. The failure mode is predictable: the app’s notifications get buried, a text sits unread for six hours, and a lead who would have converted moves on. Before you publish a forwarding number anywhere, send yourself a call and a text from another phone and confirm both arrive the way you expect, with notifications on.

Then make sure one number, the one you actually answer, is the number everywhere. Your brokerage roster, your MLS profile, your business cards, your sign riders, your email signature, and your social profiles should all carry the same line. Two numbers in circulation is worse than an out-of-area code, because the one you check less becomes a slow leak of missed opportunities you never find out about.

The question nobody asks early enough: whose number is it?

Here is the part of this topic that actually matters years later. If your brokerage issues you a business line, that number generally belongs to the brokerage, and it does not come with you when you leave. Every client, past client, and referral source who has that number in their phone effectively belongs to the phone system rather than to you.

A personal cell number or a forwarding number you own yourself does come with you. For an agent building a book of business over a decade, that difference is worth more than any area code question, and it is the same class of question as everything else in how portable your book of business is when you switch brokerages. Ask any brokerage you are considering which arrangement applies before you hand the number to five hundred people.

The honest bottom line

A local area code is a small marketing advantage in a narrow slice of your business, not a requirement and not a barrier. If you want one, it is free and it takes an afternoon. If you would rather keep the number you have had for fifteen years, keep it, and put your energy into the things that actually decide whether a stranger takes your call: whether they have heard of you, whether someone they trust sent you, and whether your brokerage’s name means something in this market.

What you should not do is publish a number anywhere without your brokerage’s licensed name beside it. That is the only part of this the state has an opinion about.
