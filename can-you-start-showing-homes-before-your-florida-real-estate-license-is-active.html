Can You Start Showing Homes Before Your Florida Real Estate License Is Fully Active?

Home›Become a Real Estate Agent in Florida›Showing Homes Before Activation

Updated July 2026 · Reviewed by Adams, Cameron & Co.

Quick answer

No. Passing the Florida real estate exam gets your license issued, but it’s placed in an inactive status until a sponsoring broker formally activates it with the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR). Until that status change goes through, you’re not legally allowed to perform any licensed activity, including showing a home, even informally or for free.

Key takeaways
  • Passing the exam issues your Florida license, but it starts in inactive status, not ready to use yet.
  • A sponsoring broker must file your license placement with DBPR before you can legally show homes or work with clients.
  • Having a broker lined up isn't the same as being activated; processing the paperwork takes time.
  • Practicing on an inactive license, even as a favor, puts the new license you just earned at risk.
  • Confirm your active status directly with DBPR or your brokerage rather than assuming you're cleared.

What does it mean when your Florida license is issued but not yet active?

When you pass the Florida real estate exam and your background check clears, the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) issues your real estate license. That’s a real milestone, but it isn’t the same as being able to work. A newly issued Florida license typically comes back in an inactive status until it’s formally placed with a sponsoring broker. Inactive means the license exists and is valid, but you aren’t authorized to perform any licensed activity under it yet.

Can you show homes before your license is active?

No. Showing a home to a buyer, discussing price or terms, negotiating on someone’s behalf, marketing a listing, or otherwise representing any side of a transaction are all licensed activities under Florida law. You cannot legally perform any of them, including a simple showing, until your license has been activated under a sponsoring broker. This applies even if you already passed the exam, even if a friend or family member asked you to help them buy a house, and even if you don’t plan to accept payment for it.

How does license activation actually work?

Activation happens when a licensed Florida broker agrees to sponsor you and submits the paperwork placing your license with their brokerage. Once DBPR processes that placement and your status flips from inactive to active, you’re legally cleared to practice, list, show homes, and represent clients under that broker’s supervision. Until that status change goes through, you’re a licensee on paper only, not someone authorized to act on a client’s behalf.

Why does Florida require a sponsoring broker before you can practice?

The activation requirement isn’t red tape for its own sake. A sales associate’s license only works under the supervision of a broker, who is legally responsible for that associate’s transactions, disclosures, and conduct. That structure is part of how Florida protects consumers: a brand-new agent isn’t operating alone, they’re backed by a broker who reviews contracts, answers questions, and carries accountability for how the transaction is handled. Requiring activation before any client-facing work simply makes sure that supervision is actually in place before it’s needed.

What if you already have a broker lined up?

Having a broker who plans to hire you doesn’t activate your license by itself. The broker still has to file the placement with DBPR, and processing isn’t instant. Most new agents build in a short buffer, commonly anywhere from a few days to a couple of weeks, between passing the exam and seeing their license show as active. It’s worth confirming with your chosen brokerage exactly when they’ll file the paperwork, so you know when you’re actually cleared to start rather than guessing.

What happens if you show homes before activation anyway?

Practicing real estate on an inactive license is exactly the kind of issue that can create real problems, both for a brand-new agent and for the broker involved. It can jeopardize the license you just worked to earn and complicate your standing with DBPR before your career has even started. There’s no upside to getting ahead of the paperwork. The safer, and only legal, path is to wait for confirmation that your status is active before doing anything with a client, including something as simple as unlocking a door for a showing.

How do you check your license status?

Your status is public information through DBPR’s license lookup, and your sponsoring brokerage should also be able to confirm it directly once the placement goes through. Don’t rely on assumption or a verbal “you’re good to go.” Confirm the status change before you take any action that could be considered licensed activity, even a favor for someone you already know.

What should you do while you wait for activation?

The waiting period isn’t wasted time. It’s a good window to finish onboarding paperwork, get set up in the brokerage’s systems and MLS access, meet your mentor, and start reaching out to your existing network to let people know you’re about to be able to help them. That way, the moment your license goes active, you’re ready to work instead of starting from zero.
